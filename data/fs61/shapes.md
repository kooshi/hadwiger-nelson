## What a "shape" is here, and why it is the interesting object

A **lattice** is a subgroup of the plane generated by unit vectors. This census identifies one by a *canonical key* — the least denominator `M` such that `M·Λ` has whole-number coordinates, together with the Hermite normal form of `M·Λ`. **That key carries no `d`**: it names an integral shape, and several different fields can hold the same shape. What differs between them is which unit vectors *lie* in it, because that depends on the field through the equation `a² + c² + d(b² + e²) = N²`.

So the census can ask a question no single field can: **take one shape, put it in several fields, and see where it refuses three colours and where it provably does not.**

### What this table says

**The same integral shape refuses in one field and is provably 3-colourable in another.** `L54356af8` (`M = 60`) refuses over `d = 71, 95, 119, 143, 191` and `359` and is open at 431. `Lb0a7206c` refuses over 23, 71 and 191 and carries a **verified periodic 3-colouring** over 119, 239, 311, 359 and 431 — a proof that no ball of it can ever refuse there. `Ld65c8ee8` (`M = 30`) is the shape that refuses over `d = 11`, and it is periodically 3-colourable over **35, 59, 131, 179 and 371**, every one of them a `d ≡ 3 (mod 8)` field.

**So the shape is not what decides — the number of unit vectors it holds is.** `Ld65c8ee8` holds 108 unit vectors over `d = 11` and only 36 over 35, 36 over 59, 28 over 131 and 179, 20 over 371. The ℤ-module is the same; the graph on it is not, because a field with fewer unit vectors of that height puts fewer edges on the same points.

**This is also phase D's question, answered by certificates instead of by a search.** Decision G asked whether `d = 11`'s recipe transfers to 35 and 59 (item D.0). The owner cancelled phase D after R-mod8 lost, and kept D.0 as a table in phase E. Here it is: **the recipe does not transfer — the shape that refuses over 11 is provably three-colourable over both of them, and over three more besides.** That is stronger than a failed search, and it cost nothing.

**And the converse is not true either**: generator count alone does not decide. `L54356af8` refuses at `d = 143` with 36 generators and is open at `d = 431` with 36. Two lattices with the same shape and the same number of unit vectors can differ, because which vectors they are differs.

### What this table says

**No two of the 9 certified witnesses are isomorphic, and the reason is immediate: no two have the same number of points.** Every point count is distinct, so the Weisfeiler–Leman hashes were never even needed to separate them; they are recorded so that a later witness can be compared against these without recomputing anything.

**And that is a fact about the extraction, not about the fields.** A witness's size is the output of a shrinking procedure that was *stopped on a budget* in four of the nine cases (the table says which). Two fields could hold essentially the same graph and still produce witnesses of 573 and 1,004 points because one deletion ran longer than the other. **The honest conclusion is that this comparison cannot decide the question it was set, and the shape table above is where the cross-field structure actually shows up.**

**The `N`-family the plan names.** Every field `d = N² − c²` owns the layer vector `(c/N, √d/N)`; for `N = 12` that family is `d = 23, 95, 119, 143`, and **all four are landed**. Phase A found that the witness lattices of `(95, 119)` share an integral shape, as do those of `(23, 191)`, with generator counts that agree exactly while the generator *sets* differ. That shared shape with equal counts is the precondition for one abstract graph drawn over several fields — and Table 1 shows what actually happens when you try it: the same shape holds a different number of unit vectors in each field, and where the count falls the lattice becomes three-colourable by certificate.

## Table 3 — [Sokolov2018]'s adjacent question, answered by a rank

Sokolov's `χ_√ℚ(ℝ²)` is the supremum over `n` of `χ(ℚ × √n·ℚ)`, and he calls the value 3 *"quite plausible"*. A 4-chromatic unit-distance graph inside such a **rank-2 layer** would refute that. The plan asks whether any certified witness, or any refusing lattice, lies in one — *up to rotation*.

**It is settled by a rank, with no search.** A point `(a, b, c, e, m)` here is `((a + b√d)/m, (c + e√d)/m)`, so `F²` is `ℚ⁴` and the layer `ℚ × √d·ℚ` is the 2-dimensional subspace `b = c = 0`. Any *rotation* of it is some other 2-dimensional ℚ-subspace. So a point set whose ℚ-span is all of `ℚ⁴` lies in no 2-dimensional subspace whatever, rotated or not.

**Every one of the 9 certified witnesses has ℚ-rank 4** (column above, computed in exact rational arithmetic), and **every refusing lattice in this census has rank 4** by construction — rank is recorded per lattice and the census never scans below it. **So nothing this milestone produced bears on Sokolov's question**, which is the same answer FS-58 gave informally ("their coordinates mix 1 and √d in both slots") now made exact and checkable.
