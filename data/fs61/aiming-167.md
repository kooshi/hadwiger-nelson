## Why this field

This project's local–global conjecture predicts a field's plane needs as many colours as its best finite residue plane allows. For `ℚ(√167)` that bound is **at least 6** — its usable residue planes are the ones at 23, 43 and 59, and five colours are refused at 23 by an exact certificate — while what is **proved** about `χ(ℚ(√167)²)` is **3**. A gap of three colours at a field of discriminant 668 is the cheapest exposed prediction the conjecture has left, which is why the repository calls this the falsification target.

## The three readings FS-59 should carry

### 1. The two balls that coloured in under two seconds could not have refused, and that is about the radius, not the field

At tier 1b two of 167's balls were 3-coloured in 0.63 s and 1.46 s, on 314,177 and 397,821 points. That looks like a strong negative and is not one. Those balls carry **481,082 and 609,374 edges — an average degree of 3.06** — and a graph that sparse has nothing to force with: almost all of it is tree-like, and a greedy colouring walks straight through. **The population that number describes is a ball at radius 1.0 on a fine lattice**, where the unit-distance neighbours of a point mostly fall outside the ball.

**The one informative ask at 167 is the radius-1.2 ball on `L3e1c94c6`** — 397,817 points and **1,688,370 edges, an average degree of 8.5** — and it is undecided: 605 s at tier 1b, and phase E re-asked it at a 3,600 s wall (see the result below). **167 has still never been asked a question that was both decidable and capable of refusing.**

### 2. The wall is the solver, not the point cap — measured, with the memory figures

Across the whole family at tier 1b, **24 of 37 asks ran to the 600-second wall undecided**, 12 were coloured and 1 refused. The balls tier 1 could not ask are on *fine* lattices — small covolume, many generators — so they are big **and dense**: 277,000–595,000 points carrying 0.7–3.3 million edges. Raising the point cap (the plan's original tier 2, at 3,000,000 points) builds **larger instances of exactly the graphs already undecided**, and the owner struck it for that reason.

**The memory that costs**, measured on every tier-1b ball: the largest ball asked was 595,257 points / 2,370,078 edges and its worker peaked at **1.93 GB**; the largest peak over all 37 was **2.47 GB**; per million points the peak ran **3.37 GB (median) to 5.22 GB (max)**. At a 3,000,000-point cap that is **10–16 GB a worker**, so a pool on a 128 GB machine is six to eight wide, not sixteen — and that figure is a floor, because finer lattices carry more edges per point and memory here tracks edges more closely than points.

### 3. What the size law predicts, and why the prediction is weak

Fitting `log(smallest refusing ball) = a + b·log d` over the **11 landed fields with `d ≡ 7 (mod 8)`** gives slope **1.10** (95 % interval -0.27 to 2.47, R² **0.22**). **R-size is scored `not decided`.**

**No prediction for `d = 167` is given here, and that is the finding.** Extrapolating the fit would read ≈ 94,645 points, but its 95 % interval runs 87 to 103,090,434 — **6 orders of magnitude**. A number with that interval is not a budget, and quoting the midpoint of it would be a prediction the data do not support.

**The data go further than "not decided": they contradict the premise.** The reading behind R-size was that `ℚ(√167)` is *not arithmetically special, only large* — that the refusing ball grows with `d`. Among the landed fields the smallest refusing ball is **3,565 points at `d = 47`** and the largest **1,803,077 at `d = 431`**, with `d = 359` refusing on 46,421 points against 248,213 at `d = 23`. **Size does not track `d`; it tracks the lattice.** What sets a ball's point count is its covolume (`π²R⁴/covolume`), and which lattice a field happens to own is arithmetic, not size.

**So 167 should not be budgeted as "the same problem, bigger".** It should be budgeted by its lattices, and the table above says what those are: one lattice reaches a degree capable of refusing under a 2,000,000-point cap, it was asked, and it coloured.

## What A.7 says about which of 167's lattices can be live — and it says nothing

Phase A item A.7 set out to find the rule behind the periodic 3-colourings that kill lattices, so that a criterion could replace a search over 24 random quotients. **It found no rule.** The smallest hidden groups are of orders 27, 108, 108, 243, 1728, 2187 and 4096, the commonest covers 29 % of dead lattices against the 90 % the gate asked for, and one of them — `167 q1+1008.1` — factors through `(ℤ/8)⁴`, an order with **no factor of 3 at all**, which kills the natural candidate (that the map is the kernel barrier's shape with a bigger 3-group in place of `𝔽₃`). And A.7's orders are **upper bounds**: one solver colouring per (lattice, modulus), so a small reading is evidence and a large one is not.

**So there is no shortcut: which of 167's lattices are live can only be settled by the quotient screen, and it has been — six of its lattices survive it.**

## The budget this implies, and the three things worth spending it on

1. **Decide the radius-1.2 ball on `L3e1c94c6`** (397,817 points, 1,688,370 edges). It is the only question at 167 that is both capable of refusing and anywhere near reach. If a long wall does not decide it, cube-and-conquer is the named next instrument — and it should be **priced on a sample of cubes before it is committed**, as M63 phase H2 priced its 552.

2. **Go deeper rather than bigger.** Tier 2l (this phase) builds lattices from unit vectors of denominator up to 420 instead of 180 at the same cap. Whatever it finds at 167 is in `census.md`; the general lesson from tier 1b is that at fixed depth the solver is the binding constraint, so the next useful move is a *different* lattice, not a bigger ball of the same one.

3. **Carry the shape table.** `data/fs61/shapes.md` lists every integral shape that refuses somewhere and every field that holds it. Three of 167's open lattices carry shapes that refuse at other fields — that is the cheapest source of a candidate this census produced, and it costs nothing to try first.

## What would falsify what

* A refusing ball at 167 proves `χ(ℚ(√167)²) ≥ 4` and moves the field from 3 to 4 — still two short of the conjecture's prediction, and the first real test of it above the level the census settles.

* A **proof** that some lattice of 167 is 3-colourable (a periodic colouring, as the quotient screen produces) closes that lattice for good and says nothing about the field.

* Nothing in this census, and nothing a census of this shape can produce, is evidence that three colours suffice over `ℚ(√167)`. **"Not landed" is a statement about a search depth.**
