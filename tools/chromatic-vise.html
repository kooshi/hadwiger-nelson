The Hadwiger–Nelson problem · a field report · August 2026

The Chromatic Vise

Colour every point of an infinite sheet of paper so that no two points exactly one unit apart share a colour. How many colours do you need? After 76 years the answer is known to be 5, 6, or 7 — and this page explains how we are squeezing that interval from both ends at once, and where its armour is thinnest.

The rules of the game

The question is called the chromatic number of the plane, posed around 1950. The rule is brutally simple: two points at distance exactly one unit must get different colours. Closer or farther is fine. The number of colours needed is written χ(ℝ²).

Both known bounds are old and elementary — which is what makes the problem famous. A honeycomb-style tiling with 7 colours, cells sized so that no cell spans a unit and same-coloured cells never land a unit apart, shows 7 always suffice. And small finite point-sets show lower bounds: if you can draw finitely many points whose unit-distance pairs cannot be properly coloured with k colours, then the whole plane needs more than k. (A classical theorem — de Bruijn–Erdős, 1951 — says the plane's chromatic number is always witnessed by some finite set, so hunting finite graphs is not a compromise. It is the whole game.)

For 68 years the interval sat at [4, 7]. In 2018 Aubrey de Grey constructed a 1581-point set that no 4-colouring survives, moving it to [5, 7]. Nothing has moved since.

What “kernel-checked” means on this page

Every claim labelled kernel-checked has been formally proven in the Lean proof assistant and verified by its small trusted kernel, from three standard logical axioms — including both ends of the interval, 5 ≤ χ(ℝ²) ≤ 7. Search code, SAT solvers, and our own scripts are treated as untrusted: they only propose; the kernel re-derives. A bug in our tools can waste our time, but it cannot produce a false theorem.

From below · forcing colours into existence

How a handful of points corners an infinite plane

Here is the smallest famous example — the Moser spindle, seven points and eleven unit distances. Try to colour it with three colours: each of the two diamond-shapes is a pair of equilateral triangles, so once the shared hub takes a colour, the far tip of each diamond is forced to repeat it. But the two tips were placed exactly one unit apart. Three colours always break; the plane needs at least four.

De Grey’s 1581-point graph does to four colours what the spindle does to three, and it is the reason the modern interval starts at 5. We hold it kernel-checked, and years of dissecting it — plus every other known 5-forcer — found that they all share one anatomy, in two acts:

Act one · pigeonhole
A counting argument forces some same-coloured pair to appear at a specific distance, somewhere in a region — no geometry, just too many points for too few colours.
Act two · kill
A web of unit edges makes every possible location of that pair fatal. The clash must land somewhere, and everywhere is booby-trapped.

If anyone can build a finite unit-distance graph that forbids a same-coloured pair at √3 (or at 2) under five colours — a “kill gadget” — then copies of it, welded onto the seven-point shell, contradict the pigeonhole and prove χ(ℝ²) ≥ 6. One gadget. That is the entire remaining distance between here and the first movement of this problem since 2018.

From above · trying to colour the plane

Why seven refuses to become six

The upper bound is a construction, and here is ours — the one the kernel checked. Bricks of size 4/5 × 3/5, each row slid 2/5 along, painted with seven colours in a repeating pattern. Inside one brick, no two points reach distance 1 (the diagonal is exactly 1, and the bricks are half-open — a point owns its left and bottom edge only, so the two corners of that diagonal never both belong). And two bricks of the same colour are always too far apart for their points to touch at distance 1. Seven colours, whole plane, no clashes. It is boundary-tight: the construction works with zero slack, everywhere.

To do better than seven, one natural family of ideas suggests itself: keep colouring by cells of a lattice, but tune the knobs — shrink the cells, change the pattern, change the lattice spacing. Over the last six milestones we turned every knob in that family, and each door closed as a theorem, not as a failed experiment:

Lattice patterns
Colour by “nearest lattice point, then pattern” — every such scheme, at any scale, any sublattice, any tie-breaking, needs exactly seven colours. Kernel-checked.
Any coarse cells
Drop the pattern entirely: any colouring constant on cells bigger than half the unit distance needs seven. Kernel-checked.
Finer cells
Intuition says smaller cells approximate the plane better, so they should do no worse. The intuition is false — and this is where it gets strange.

The accented bands deserve a closer look, because they are the strangest objects this programme has produced. Normally, when a graph needs many colours, you can point at the reason: a clique, a cluster of mutually-clashing points, and a graph with a k-clique trivially needs k colours. The classical window works exactly like that — seven colours, and a seven-clique to blame. But just below it sits a band whose graphs need seven colours while their largest clique has only four points. Every standard estimation tool — clique counting, eigenvalue bounds, fractional relaxations — reads “about 4” there. The truth is 7. The chinks in the armour

Where the vise bites next

Honesty first: the interval has not narrowed since 2018, and nothing guarantees 6 is even part of the answer — the truth may simply be 5, or 7. But the campaign has reduced “make progress” to three sharply-posed targets, ranked.

Chink 1 · from below

One gadget closes χ ≥ 6

The reduction from Figure 3: find any finite unit-distance graph that, under five colours, forces two points at distance √3 (or 2) to differ. Nine copies on the seven-point shell then finish the proof by pure composition — the assembly technique is already built and was used to weld a 4,749-point five-forcer from parts.

Status: the detector for such gadgets exists and has been run over ~285,000 points of candidate material — every reading so far is zero. Next sweeps: differently-anchored material, deliberately detuned tilings, deeper closures. Absence-of-signal here is itself a finding about where the gadget can’t live.

Chink 2 · from above

The sharpest open scale: p = 1/7

One cell scale, seven times finer than the classical one, refuses to answer. Finite patches up to radius 225 happily accept six colours; wrap-around (periodic) colourings refuse six at every period tried up to 36. One of those two signals must break. A six-colouring found at this scale — or any scale — is a six-colouring of the plane: χ(ℝ²) ≤ 6, immediately. A refutation instead closes the sharpest gap in the spectrum, and promotes to a kernel theorem the same day through the pipeline that certified Figure 6.

Status: next campaign (M43), stubbed and ready — bigger patches, longer periods, then the wholly unprobed tail below p ≈ 0.224.

Chink 3 · the mechanism

Learn to see the invisible forcing

At the gap bands, every known lower-bound instrument reads 4 while the truth is 7. Whatever is forcing those three extra colours is a mechanism nobody can yet certify directly — and it is the same shape as the plane’s own mystery, where the largest clique has been a triangle since 1950 and the truth is at least 5. The 23-point core of Figure 6 is now the smallest laboratory animal carrying the disease: find the certificate that sees its gap, and you likely hold a new instrument for the plane itself.

Status: open theory. The autopsy of five such cores says the forcing always factors as pigeonhole × kill; what is missing is a certificate language for that composition.
