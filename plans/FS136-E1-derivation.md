**The stall read.** Three of its shapes at once. *A result proved only at instances*: the count is an entry for two disjoint

**For a stranger** (a convex geometer who knows nothing of this project). *Given*: finitely many connected open sets `P_j` in
the plane, each with a label out of `k`; a point `w`; a measurable labelling of a neighbourhood of `w`, such that for each
`j` almost no point `z` whose unit circle meets `P_j` has the label of `P_j`. *Unknown*: which labels can occur near `w`, and
where. *Condition*: almost every point near `w` has some label. Since `P_j` is connected, the unit circle about `z` misses
`P_j` exactly when `P_j` lies in the open unit disc about `z` or outside the closed one. **This is a covering problem**: the
label of `P_j` is available at `z` only if `z` lies in the *ball-polar* of `P_j` (the intersection of the unit discs about
its points) or outside its *unit parallel body*; near `w` the boundary of that set is made of arcs of unit circles about the
points of `cl(P_j)` at distance exactly 1 from `w`; and the condition is that the available sets of the `k` labels cover a
neighbourhood of `w` up to a null set. Words the reader would stop at: "pieced point", "covering pair", "ring", "point
across" — each struck or defined: a pieced point is a point of several closures; a ring is a unit circle; the point across is
`x + y − p`. Nothing is left that the reader's field has no word for.

**The homes, each tested.**

1. *This project's own* — FS110-N-SEAM with N-E2 and FS110-CALC. Dictionary: tile ↔ piece; "Siamese or outer" ↔ inner or
   outer; `P_cl(u)` ↔ the labels of the family at `u`; covering pair ↔ covering pair. The proofs apply with **one changed
   step**: N-E0 (two tile points one unit apart) is replaced by C124-SHADOW-OPEN (Fubini on an open one-label set), because
   at `w` there is no tile — the conclusion becomes "almost every point near `w`". The exceptions vanish: three-piece, full,
   sector and bowtie members are one statement at the level of the table. It predicts a neighbour: a point with three
   members and six cells (the strip's top-edge point). **Adopted.**
2. *Covering a circle by arcs* (elementary combinatorial geometry). To first order each member's circle cuts the directions
   at `w` into two open half-circles, each label's available directions are an intersection of such half-circles — an open
   arc of length at most 180°, or nothing — and the condition is that the labels' arcs cover the circle of directions less
   the `2m` tangent directions. Its (trivial) theorem — arcs of length at most 180° cover a circle only if two are
   complementary half-circles or there are at least three — forces: with every label constrained, **two labels suffice at
   `w` only when they are split along one member's circle**, and otherwise three are present. C124-FORCED-PIECES's identity
   `(A ∨ C)(A ∨ D)(B ∨ C)(B ∨ D) = AB ∨ CD` is the cover of four cells. **Adopted as the description under which the count is
   forced**: the table is a cover of cells by arcs. Its limit, named: directions are first order; a member antipodal to
   another about `w` gives tangent circles, and a piece's side is decided by its points, not its cone.
3. *Ball-polar duality and spindle convexity* (convex geometry; Mayer's "Überkonvexität", Bezdek–Lángi–Naszódi–Papez's

   whose tiles have diameter exactly 1: a tile of constant width bars its label everywhere within distance 1 of it but on
   the tile itself. Its theorems decide nothing about a cover by six. **Kept as a dictionary for the exact computations,
   killed as a deciding home.** Nothing below rests on the recalled theorem.
