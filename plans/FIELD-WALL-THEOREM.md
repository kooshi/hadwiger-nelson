# The field-wall conjecture: four colours suffice for unit distances over ℚ(√3, √11)

*Standalone write-up, 2026-09-05. Written for a reader who knows the Hadwiger–Nelson problem but has
no knowledge of the computational programme in which this question arose. Sections 1–4 state the
question as it was registered. Section 5 gives a proof that the conjecture is true, found in the course
of writing this note; the proof is elementary algebraic number theory and every step can be checked
by hand. Sections 6–8 draw the consequences and list what is still open.*

---

## 1. Summary

Let K = ℚ(√3, √11) ⊂ ℝ, and regard K² as a subset of the Euclidean plane. Join two points of K²
when their Euclidean distance is exactly 1. The **field-wall conjecture** asserts that this graph is
4-colourable — equivalently, that every finite unit-distance graph all of whose vertices have both
coordinates in K admits a proper colouring with four colours.

The number 4 is sharp: the Moser spindle, the classical 7-vertex unit-distance graph with chromatic
number 4, can be drawn with all coordinates in K, and K is the *smallest* field in which it can be
drawn at all (§4). Every published unit-distance graph with chromatic number 5 uses coordinates
outside K — de Grey's needs √5 and √7, Heule's need √5, Haugland's needs a cubic irrationality —
even though their "fabric" is built from the same hexagonal and seam-rotation material that lives in
K. The conjecture says this is not an accident: five colours are never forced inside K.

**The conjecture is true.** Section 5 proves that the unit-distance graph on K² has chromatic number
exactly 4, by an "additive" colouring in the tradition of Woodall's proof that the rational plane is
2-colourable: colour a point by its residue modulo a suitable prime ideal 𝔭 of K(i) lying above 2.
The two facts that make this work are (i) the prime 𝔭 is fixed by complex conjugation, so every unit
vector is a 𝔭-adic unit and a unit step always changes the residue, and (ii) its residue field is
𝔽₄, so four residues suffice. The same argument locates the wall exactly: it stands for a real field F
precisely when −1 fails to be a square in some 2-adic completion of F, and adjoining √5 or √7 to K is
exactly what makes −1 a 2-adic square (−15 ≡ −7 ≡ 1 mod 8). That is why the known 5-chromatic
graphs need those roots, and why √2, √10, √14, √35, √70 — also reachable from K by one geometric
construction step — can never help.

For the programme that raised the question (a descent that replaces distance-2 edges of a 6-chromatic
two-distance graph by unit-distance material, all of it so far inside K), the consequence is a
theorem rather than a risk: no sequence of constructions confined to K² can ever produce a
non-4-colourable unit-distance graph, hence can never terminate. The material that can break the wall
is characterised in §6.

## 2. Setting and notation

**Unit-distance graphs.** For a set S ⊆ ℝ², the unit-distance graph G(S) has vertex set S and an
edge between x and y whenever |x − y| = 1. The Hadwiger–Nelson problem asks for χ(ℝ²) := χ(G(ℝ²)).
Known: 5 ≤ χ(ℝ²) ≤ 7 (de Grey 2018 for the lower bound; the hexagonal 7-colouring for the upper).
By the de Bruijn–Erdős theorem (assuming the axiom of choice), χ(G(S)) is the supremum of χ(G(T))
over finite T ⊆ S, so statements about "every finite unit-distance graph in S" and about χ(G(S))
are interchangeable.

**The plane over a field.** For a subfield F ⊆ ℝ we write F² for {(x, y) : x, y ∈ F} and identify
it with the set {x + iy} ⊂ ℂ, which is the field F(i). Under this identification |z|² = z·z̄, where
the bar is complex conjugation, the nontrivial element of Gal(F(i)/F). The set of unit vectors

    U(F) := { u ∈ F(i) : u·ū = 1 }

is a multiplicative group (the norm-one torus of F(i)/F; by Hilbert 90 it equals {w/w̄ : w ∈ F(i)×}).
The unit-distance graph on F² is the Cayley graph of the additive group F(i) with connection set
U(F): x ~ y iff y − x ∈ U(F). It is invariant under translation by F(i), under multiplication by
U(F) (the rotations defined over F) and under conjugation.

Two classical data points. Woodall (1973) proved χ(G(ℚ²)) = 2. Fischer (1990) introduced *additive*
k-colourings of F^d — group homomorphisms from the subgroup Λ(F) ⊂ F(i) generated by U(F) to a
group of order k, nonzero on every unit vector — and studied which fields admit them. The colouring
constructed in §5 is additive in this sense. Johnson (1987) studied 2-colourings of real quadratic
extensions of ℚ²; neither text was accessible while writing this, so whether Theorem B or the
criterion of §6 appears there is unchecked.

**The field K.** K = ℚ(√3, √11) is the biquadratic field with ℚ-basis 1, √3, √11, √33
(√33 = √3·√11). Its three quadratic subfields are ℚ(√3), ℚ(√11), ℚ(√33). A rational number is a
square in K iff it lies in {1, 3, 11, 33}·ℚ×²; in particular √2, √5, √7, √15 ∉ K.

**Elements of U(K) that will recur.** All of the following have both coordinates in K and modulus 1:

| name | value | cos, sin | role |
|---|---|---|---|
| ζ₆ | (1 + i√3)/2 | 1/2, √3/2 | 60° rotation; the equilateral triangle |
| ζ₁₂ | (√3 + i)/2 | √3/2, 1/2 | 30° rotation |
| ρ | (5 + i√11)/6 | 5/6, √11/6 | the Moser twist: the rotation that closes the Moser spindle |
| σ | (√3 + i√33)/6 | √3/6, √33/6 | the "seam" rotation: carries a point of the unit circle about 0 to a unit-distance neighbour on the circle of radius 1/√3 (σ/√3 = (1 + i√11)/6 is at distance 1 from 1) |
| τ₅ | (49 + 3i√11)/50 | 49/50, 3√11/50 | the rotation taking a point at distance 5 to a point one unit away |

Two identities worth recording: ρ·σ² = −1 (direct computation: σ² = (−5 + i√11)/6), so the Moser
twist is a word in the group ⟨ζ₆, σ⟩; and σ has infinite order, since 2·Re σ = 1/√3 is not an
algebraic integer, so σ is not a root of unity.

## 3. The conjecture as registered

> **Conjecture (field wall).** Every finite unit-distance graph whose vertices all lie in K² is
> properly 4-colourable. Equivalently, χ(G(K²)) = 4.

Equivalent formulations:

1. The Cayley graph Cay(K(i), U(K)) is 4-colourable.
2. There is no 5-chromatic unit-distance graph with all coordinates in K — in *any* placement; the
   statement is about the field of the coordinates, not about a graph up to isometry.
3. (Via the spare-colour lemma, §7.) No unit-distance graph in K² forces two vertices to receive equal
   colours in every proper 4-colouring while remaining 4-colourable itself — more precisely, a
   4-colourable graph admits no colour compulsion between a pair of vertices at 5 colours, so the
   conjecture is equivalent to: no finite K-point unit-distance graph carries a forced-equal or
   forced-distinct pair at five colours.

The lower bound χ ≥ 4 is the Moser spindle (§4). The content of the conjecture is the upper bound.

**Why this field.** Three independent lines of evidence made K the natural place to ask:

* every published 5-chromatic unit-distance graph leaves K, and does so through exactly the rotation
  that closes its forcing (§6.3);
* the infinite "seam lattices" — countable K-point unit-distance graphs closed under all unit chords,
  built from the group ⟨ζ₆, σ⟩ acting on circles of radius 1 and 1/√3 about one, two, four and seven
  centres — were proved (machine-checked, in Lean 4 with Mathlib) to have chromatic number exactly 4;
* a computational descent (§7) that repeatedly adjoins to a K-point graph the points at unit distance
  from two existing points, choosing them adversarially to destroy colourings, produced K-point
  unit-distance graphs with ~2,000 vertices and ~14,000 edges that remained 4-colourable, while the
  same material with a *single* copy rotated by an angle outside K (cos 7/8, sin √15/8) refused four
  colours at once.

## 4. The lower bound, and why K is the Moser field

**The Moser spindle in K².** Take the rhombus R = {0, ζ₆, ζ₃, i√3} (two unit equilateral triangles
sharing the edge from ζ₆ = (1+i√3)/2 to ζ₃ = (−1+i√3)/2; the far corner is ζ₆ + ζ₃ = i√3) and its
image ρ·R under the Moser twist. The seven vertices

    0,  ζ₆,  ζ₃,  i√3,  ρζ₆ = (5 − √33 + i(5√3 + √11))/12,  ρζ₃ = (−5 − √33 + i(5√3 − √11))/12,  ρ·i√3 = (−√33 + 5i√3)/6

all lie in K². The eleven edges are the ten rhombus edges and the segment from i√3 to ρ·i√3, whose
length is √3·|1 − ρ| = √3·√((1/6)² + 11/36) = 1. This is the Moser spindle, so χ(G(K²)) ≥ 4.

**K is the smallest field containing a Moser spindle.**

*Lemma 4.1.* If F ⊆ ℝ is a field and F² contains an equilateral triangle of side 1, then √3 ∈ F.
If F² contains a Moser spindle, then √3, √11 ∈ F, hence K ⊆ F.

*Proof.* If a, b ∈ F² are two vertices of a unit equilateral triangle, the third vertex is
(a + b)/2 ± i(√3/2)(b − a); since b − a ∈ F² is nonzero, this lies in F² iff √3 ∈ F. For the
spindle: the long diagonals u, u′ ∈ F(i) of the two rhombi satisfy |u|² = |u′|² = 3 and
|u − u′| = 1, so Re(u ū′) = 5/2 and Im(u ū′) = ±√(9 − 25/4) = ±√11/2; both are coordinates of
u ū′ ∈ F(i), so √11 ∈ F. ∎

So among fields admitting the classical 4-forcer, K is minimal, and the conjecture asks whether the
*next* step — five colours — is possible in the same field. (Other 4-chromatic unit-distance graphs,
e.g. triangle-free ones, live in other fields; the claim is minimality among fields containing a
Moser spindle, nothing more.)

## 5. The conjecture is a theorem

### 5.1 Residue colourings

*Theorem A (residue colouring).* Let F ⊂ ℝ be a number field, L = F(i), and let 𝔭 be a nonzero
prime ideal of the ring of integers O_L with 𝔭̄ = 𝔭 (𝔭 is fixed by complex conjugation). Then the
unit-distance graph on F² is properly colourable with N(𝔭) = |O_L/𝔭| colours. Hence

    χ(G(F²)) ≤ min { N(𝔭) : 𝔭 a prime of F(i) with 𝔭̄ = 𝔭 }.

*Proof.* Let v = v_𝔭 be the 𝔭-adic valuation on L and O_(𝔭) = {z ∈ L : v(z) ≥ 0} the local ring at
𝔭, a subring of L with residue map r : O_(𝔭) → O_L/𝔭 =: 𝔽, a ring homomorphism.

(i) *Every unit vector is a 𝔭-adic unit.* For u ∈ U(F), u·ū = 1 gives v(u) + v(ū) = 0. For any
automorphism g of L, v_{g(𝔭)}(g(z)) = v_𝔭(z); applied to g = conjugation, v_𝔭(ū) = v_{𝔭̄}(u) =
v_𝔭(u). Hence 2v(u) = 0, so u ∈ O_(𝔭)× and r(u) ≠ 0.

(ii) *The colouring on O_(𝔭).* Colour z ∈ O_(𝔭) by r(z) ∈ 𝔽. If z ~ w then w − z = u ∈ U(F), and
r(w) − r(z) = r(u) ≠ 0. So r is a proper colouring of G(O_(𝔭)) with |𝔽| = N(𝔭) colours.

(iii) *Extension to F².* Since U(F) ⊆ O_(𝔭), every edge of G(F²) joins two points of the same coset
of the additive subgroup O_(𝔭) ⊂ L. Choose a representative z_C for each coset C and colour
z_C + t by r(t). Within a coset, z_C + t ~ z_C + t′ iff t′ − t ∈ U(F), so the colours differ. (For
the finite-graph form of the statement no choice is needed: a finite graph meets finitely many
cosets.) ∎

*Remarks.* (a) Restricted to the subgroup Λ(F) generated by U(F), the colouring is the additive group
homomorphism r : Λ(F) → 𝔽, nonzero on U(F): an additive N(𝔭)-colouring in Fischer's sense. (b) For
F = ℚ, L = ℚ(i), 𝔭 = (1 + i) is the unique prime above 2, 𝔭̄ = 𝔭, and O_L/𝔭 = 𝔽₂: Theorem A is
Woodall's 2-colouring of ℚ² (a unit vector (a + bi)/c with a² + b² = c² has c odd and a + b odd,
so it is ≡ 1 mod (1 + i)). (c) The hypothesis 𝔭̄ = 𝔭 is essential and is exactly where the "wall"
will turn out to live.

### 5.2 The prime above 2 in K(i)

*Lemma 5.2.* Let L = K(i) = ℚ(i, √3, √11), a Galois extension of ℚ of degree 8 with group
Gal(L/ℚ) ≅ (ℤ/2)³ generated by c (i ↦ −i), s₃ (√3 ↦ −√3), s₁₁ (√11 ↦ −√11). Then
2·O_L = (𝔭₁𝔭₂)² with 𝔭₁ ≠ 𝔭₂, each 𝔭ⱼ has residue field O_L/𝔭ⱼ ≅ 𝔽₄, and c(𝔭ⱼ) = 𝔭ⱼ for j = 1, 2.

*Proof.* (a) *Local structure.* ℚ₂(i)/ℚ₂ is ramified of degree 2, and ℚ₂(√−3) = ℚ₂(ζ₃) is the
unramified quadratic extension of ℚ₂ (x² + x + 1 is irreducible over 𝔽₂). Their compositum
ℚ₂(ζ₁₂) = ℚ₂(i, √3) therefore has degree 4 over ℚ₂ with ramification index e = 2, residue degree
f = 2 and residue field 𝔽₄. Since 33 ≡ 1 (mod 8), 33 is a square in ℚ₂, so √11 = √33/√3 already
lies in ℚ₂(i, √3). Hence L ⊗_ℚ ℚ₂ ≅ ℚ₂(ζ₁₂) × ℚ₂(ζ₁₂): the prime 2 has exactly g = 8/4 = 2 primes
above it in L, each with e = 2 and f = 2, i.e. residue field 𝔽₄.

(b) *Conjugation fixes them.* L/ℚ is abelian, so the decomposition groups of 𝔭₁ and 𝔭₂ coincide;
call this subgroup D. It has order ef = 4, and its fixed field Z is the unique quadratic subfield of
L in which 2 splits completely. The quadratic subfields of L are ℚ(√d) for d ∈ {−1, 3, −3, 11, −11,
33, −33}, and 2 splits in ℚ(√d) (d squarefree) iff d ≡ 1 (mod 8); only d = 33 qualifies. So
Z = ℚ(√33), and D = Gal(L/ℚ(√33)) = {g : g(√33) = √33}. Complex conjugation fixes the real number
√33, so c ∈ D, i.e. c(𝔭₁) = 𝔭₁ and c(𝔭₂) = 𝔭₂. ∎

*A concrete cross-check.* The two primes correspond to the two 2-adic square roots ±s of 33; take
s ∈ ℤ₂ with s ≡ 17 (mod 256) (indeed 17² = 289 = 33 + 256, and Hensel lifts). Then 𝔭₁ is the prime
above 2 at which √33 − 17 has large valuation (its image is s − 17, divisible by 2⁸) and 𝔭₂ the one
at which it has small valuation (image −s − 17 = −(s + 17), exactly divisible by 2). Conjugation
fixes √33 − 17 and preserves valuations along the ideal it moves, so it cannot exchange the two primes.

### 5.3 The theorem

*Theorem B.* χ(G(K²)) = 4 for K = ℚ(√3, √11). Equivalently: every finite unit-distance graph whose
vertices have both coordinates in ℚ(√3, √11) is properly 4-colourable, and some such graph (the Moser
spindle) needs four colours.

*Proof.* Theorem A with F = K and 𝔭 = 𝔭₁ from Lemma 5.2 gives a proper colouring with
N(𝔭₁) = |𝔽₄| = 4 colours; §4 gives the lower bound. ∎

The colouring is explicit. Fix the 2-adic embedding L ↪ ℚ₂(ζ₁₂) with √33 ↦ s (s ≡ 17 mod 256). The
ring of integers of ℚ₂(ζ₁₂) is R[i] with R = ℤ₂[ζ₃], a uniformiser is 1 − i, and the residue map is

    R[i] → R/2R ≅ 𝔽₄,   a + b·i ↦ (a + b) mod 2.

A point z ∈ K² = L is coloured by writing its image as a + b·i with a, b ∈ R (possible exactly when z
is 𝔭₁-integral; every unit vector is, by Theorem A(i)) and reading (a + b) mod 2 ∈ 𝔽₄ = 𝔽₂(ω),
ω = ζ₃ mod 2. Some values, using √3 = −i(2ζ₃ + 1) and √11 = −(s/3)·i·(2ζ₃ + 1):

| u | image as a + b·i | residue r(u) |
|---|---|---|
| ζ₆ = 1 + ζ₃ | a = 1 + ζ₃, b = 0 | 1 + ω = ω² |
| ρ = (5 + i√11)/6 | a = (8t′ + sζ₃)/9 with 15 + s = 16t′, b = 0 | ω |
| σ = (√3 + i√33)/6 | a = 0, b = (8t − ζ₃)/3 with s − 1 = 16t | ω² |
| τ₅ = (49 + 3i√11)/50 | a = ((49 + s)/2 + sζ₃)/25, b = 0 | ω² |
| 2u, for any unit vector u | — | 0 |

Every unit vector has nonzero residue, as Theorem A(i) guarantees, so a unit step always changes the
colour; a step of length 2 never does.

### 5.4 Consistency checks

The proof is short enough that its reliability is best judged against everything already known.

* **Woodall.** For F = ℚ the same construction is Woodall's theorem (Remark (b) above).
* **Distance 2 is monochromatic.** Since 2 ∈ 𝔭, the colouring gives every pair of points at distance
  2 the same colour, and likewise every pair at distance 4, 2√3, 2/√3, 8/3, 8/√3, … (any distance
  of positive 𝔭-adic valuation). This is exactly as it must be: Exoo and Ismailescu's 6-chromatic
  {1, 2}-distance graph has all its 426 vertices in K² (their coordinates are
  ((a√3 + b√11)/12, (c + d√33)/12) with integers a, b, c, d), so no proper 4-colouring of G(K²) can
  avoid monochromatic pairs at distance 2 — and this one avoids none of them. Separately, the
  programme had proved (machine-checked) that every proper 4-colouring of the plane contains a
  monochromatic pair at distance 2, and had measured, on its ~2,000-point K-point graphs, that the
  only pairs forced equal in every 4-colouring sit at distances 8/3 and 8/√3 — precisely distances of
  positive 𝔭-adic valuation, which the residue colouring makes monochromatic.
* **Distance √3 is never monochromatic.** r(√3·u) = r(√3)·r(u) ≠ 0. Together with the previous item,
  the closed hexagonal shell (a point and its six unit neighbours, pairwise at distances 1, √3, 2)
  is coloured with the three antipodal pairs monochromatic and everything else distinct, as it must be.
* **The fields of the 5-chromatic graphs fail the hypothesis.** De Grey's 1581-vertex graph has
  coordinates in ℚ(√3, √5, √7, √11) and Heule's 553- and 510-vertex graphs in ℚ(√3, √5, √11)
  (both facts verified exactly by the programme). If either field admitted a conjugation-fixed prime
  of norm ≤ 4, Theorem A would contradict a machine-verified theorem. They do not: for
  F = ℚ(√3, √5, √11), the decomposition field of 2 in F(i) is ℚ(√33, √−15) (both 33 and −15 are
  ≡ 1 mod 8), which conjugation moves, so conjugation swaps the primes above 2; for de Grey's field
  the same happens through √−15 and √−7. The smallest conjugation-fixed prime of ℚ(√3, √5, √11, i)
  lies above 11 and has norm 121. (§6 makes this systematic.)
* **The seam lattices.** The countable ⟨ζ₆, σ⟩-lattices whose chromatic number the programme
  proved to be exactly 4 are subsets of K², so Theorem B re-proves those upper bounds uniformly —
  and explains why every such closure came out 4-chromatic rather than 5.

## 6. The wall, located

### 6.1 The criterion at 2

*Proposition 6.1.* Let F ⊂ ℝ be a number field and 𝔮 a prime of F above 2. The primes of F(i) above
𝔮 are fixed by conjugation iff 𝔮 does not split in F(i)/F, iff −1 is not a square in the completion
F_𝔮. In that case there is exactly one such prime 𝔭, and N(𝔭) = N(𝔮)^f, where f ∈ {1, 2} is the
residue degree of F_𝔮(i)/F_𝔮.

*Proof.* Conjugation generates Gal(F(i)/F) and permutes the primes above 𝔮. If there is only one
(𝔮 inert or ramified), it is fixed. If 𝔮 splits as 𝔭𝔭′, the decomposition group of 𝔭 is trivial, so
conjugation moves 𝔭 to 𝔭′. Finally 𝔮 splits in F(i) = F(√−1) iff F_𝔮(√−1) = F_𝔮 iff −1 ∈ F_𝔮×². ∎

*Corollary 6.2 (four colours).* If F has a prime 𝔮 above 2 at which −1 is not a square, with
N(𝔮) = 2, or with N(𝔮) = 4 and F_𝔮(i)/F_𝔮 ramified, then χ(G(F²)) ≤ 4.

*Corollary 6.3 (a necessary condition for five).* If F² contains a 5-chromatic unit-distance graph,
then −1 is a square in F_𝔮 for every prime 𝔮 of F above 2 with residue field 𝔽₂.

For odd primes the method is weaker by nature: a conjugation-fixed prime above an odd p has f = 2
(F_𝔮(i)/F_𝔮 is unramified for odd residue characteristic, and cannot be trivial), so its norm is a
square. Residue colourings therefore produce bounds of the form 2^a or q²; they can never show
χ(G(F²)) ≤ 5, 6 or 7.

### 6.2 Multiquadratic fields

Let F = ℚ(√d₁, …, √dₙ) be real, and S ⊂ ℚ×/ℚ×² the group generated by the dₖ. All completions of F
at primes above 2 are isomorphic to ℚ₂(√e : e ∈ S), and −1 is a square there iff −1 ∈ S·ℚ₂×², iff
some squarefree e ∈ S has −e ≡ 1 (mod 8), i.e. **iff S contains an odd element ≡ 7 (mod 8)**.

*Proposition 6.4.* If no odd element of S is ≡ 7 (mod 8), then χ(G(F²)) ≤ 4; the bound improves to 2
when the 2-adic completion of F(i) has residue field 𝔽₂ (equivalently, contains no primitive cube
root of unity).

Examples: ℚ, ℚ(√2), ℚ(√10) — two colours (ℚ(√2)² and ℚ(√10)² are bipartite); ℚ(√3), ℚ(√5),
ℚ(√11), K = ℚ(√3, √11), K(√2), K(√35) — four colours.

*Corollary 6.5 (real quadratic fields).* Let d > 1 be squarefree and F = ℚ(√d). The 2-adic completion
of F(i) contains √−1 iff d ≡ 7 (mod 8), and contains a primitive cube root of unity iff d ≡ 3 or 5
(mod 8) (ζ₃ ∈ ℚ₂(√d, i) iff −3 ∈ ⟨−1, d⟩·ℚ₂×², and −3 ≡ 5 mod 8). Hence the unit-distance graph of
F² is

* **bipartite** when d is even or d ≡ 1 (mod 8) — Woodall's colouring of ℚ² transplants (for
  d ≡ 1 mod 8 the field embeds in ℚ₂, so the same parity works verbatim);
* **at most 4-chromatic** when d ≡ 3 or 5 (mod 8) — ℚ(√3), ℚ(√5), ℚ(√11), ℚ(√13), …;
* beyond the method when d ≡ 7 (mod 8) — ℚ(√7), ℚ(√15), ℚ(√23), …, where the wall falls.

The last class is not degenerate: ℚ(√7)² contains odd cycles. With ζ = (3 + i√7)/4 (a unit vector,
9/16 + 7/16 = 1) the three steps −1, ζ, ζ sum to w = (1 + i√7)/2 with |w|² = 2, and 4/|w|² − 1 = 1
is a square, so the two unit vectors (w/2)(−1 ± i) close a 5-cycle whose vertices 0, −1, −1 + ζ,
−1 + 2ζ, −1 + 2ζ + (w/2)(−1 + i) all lie in ℚ(√7)². So χ(G(ℚ(√7)²)) ≥ 3, the field has no
equilateral triangle (√3 ∉ ℚ(√7)), and whether it is 3, 4 or more is open.

**One step beyond K.** For a squarefree integer d, the square-class group of K(√d) is
{1, 3, 11, 33}·{1, d}, and {1, 3, 11, 33} ≡ {1, 3, 3, 1} (mod 8). An odd element ≡ 7 (mod 8) appears
iff d ≡ 7 or 3d ≡ 7 (mod 8), i.e. iff d ≡ 5 or 7 (mod 8). Hence:

| adjoin to K | d mod 8 | wall | χ(G(K(√d)²)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| √2, √10, √14, √70 (d even) | — | stands | ≤ 4 |
| √35 | 3 | stands | ≤ 4 |
| √5 (equivalently √15) | 5 | **falls** | ≥ 5 (Heule's 510-vertex graph lives here) |
| √7 (equivalently √21) | 7 | **falls** | ≥ 4, open |
| √13, √23, √29, √31, … | 5 or 7 | falls | open |

The eight rational square classes a single geometric gluing step can adjoin to K (§7.3) are
{1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 14, 35, 70}; exactly two of them, 5 and 7, can take the plane past four colours. This
is the arithmetic content of the empirical "field wall": the known 5-chromatic constructions need
√5 or √7 because those are the roots that make −1 a 2-adic square.

**Spindles at even distance always break the wall.** The rotation that closes a forced pair at
distance d has sine √(4d² − 1)/(2d²). For an integer d, 4d² − 1 ≡ 7 (mod 8) iff d is even, and then
−(4d² − 1) ≡ 1 (mod 8) is a 2-adic square: adjoining √(4d² − 1) adjoins a 2-adic √−1. De Grey's two
spindle rotations are at d = 2 (sine √15/8) and d = 4 (sine 3√7/32); Haugland's is at d = 2.
Spindles at d = 1, √3 or 5 (radicands 3, 11, 99 = 9·11) stay inside K and cannot help.

### 6.2′ The linear refinement, and the 3-adic wall

Theorem A colours by the full residue class. When the residue field is larger than needed, a
subgroup of it suffices.

*Theorem A′.* Let 𝔭 be a conjugation-fixed prime of F(i) with residue field k, and let k₀ be the
residue field of 𝔮 = 𝔭 ∩ F. Every unit vector reduces into the norm-one group C = {c ∈ k× : c·c̄ = 1}
(conjugation acts on k as the k₀-Frobenius). If H ⊆ k is an additive subgroup with H ∩ C = ∅, then
z ↦ (z mod 𝔭) + H is a proper colouring of G(F²) with [k : H] colours.

*Proof.* A unit step changes the residue by an element of C, which is not in H. ∎

*Corollary 6.6 (the 3-adic wall).* If F has a prime above 3 of residue degree one — some completion
of F at 3 is ℚ₃ or a ramified extension of it — then χ(G(F²)) ≤ 3. More generally, if F has a prime
above an odd p ≡ 3 (mod 4) of residue degree one, then χ(G(F²)) ≤ p.

*Proof.* Such a 𝔮 has residue field 𝔽_p in which −1 is not a square, so 𝔮 is inert in F(i): the
prime above it is conjugation-fixed with residue field 𝔽_{p²}, and C has order p + 1. The 𝔽_p-lines
of 𝔽_{p²} number p + 1; a line through c ∈ C meets C exactly in {c, −c} (λc ∈ C forces λ² = 1), so
exactly (p + 1)/2 lines meet C and the others avoid it. Take H to be one of them: p colours. ∎

Consequences for the small fields:

* **χ(G(ℚ(√3)²)) = 3.** The prime 3 ramifies in ℚ(√3) with residue field 𝔽₃, so three colours
  suffice, and the equilateral triangle needs three.
* **χ(G(ℚ(√7)²)) = 3.** The prime 3 splits in ℚ(√7) (7 ≡ 1 mod 3); the 5-cycle of Corollary 6.5
  needs three.
* **K = ℚ(√3, √11) escapes.** Its primes above 3 have residue field 𝔽₉ (11 is not a square mod 3),
  which contains √−1, so they split in K(i) and Theorem A′ has nothing to colour with: consistent
  with the Moser spindle in K.
* **Every field containing a Moser spindle passes both walls.** χ ≥ 4 needs: no conjugation-fixed
  prime above 3 with residue field 𝔽₃ (Corollary 6.6), and — this is the content of §6.1–6.2 — a
  4-chromatic graph is still allowed behind the 2-adic wall. χ ≥ 5 needs the 2-adic wall broken as
  well. The residue method's thresholds below 7 are exactly 2, 3 and 4; 5, 6 and 7 are invisible to it.
* **The smallest quadratic candidates for χ ≥ 4 without triangles** are ℚ(√d) with d ≡ 7 (mod 8)
  (2-adic wall broken) and d ≡ 2 (mod 3) (3 inert, so no residue-𝔽₃ prime): d ≡ 23 (mod 24) —
  ℚ(√23), ℚ(√47), ℚ(√71), …. Whether any of these planes needs four colours is open; the 8/√3
  witness of §7.5 lives in ℚ(√3, √11, √23).

Define the **residue chromatic number** χ_res(F) as the least [k : H] over all conjugation-fixed
primes 𝔭 of F(i) and all additive H ⊆ k avoiding C. Then χ(G(F²)) ≤ χ_res(F), with χ_res(ℚ) = 2,
χ_res(ℚ(√3)) = χ_res(ℚ(√7)) = 3, χ_res(K) = 4 (each tight), and χ_res = 11 already for Heule's
field ℚ(√3, √5, √11) — above the plane's own 7, so the invariant says nothing there.

### 6.3 Reading the known constructions through the wall

Every published 5-chromatic unit-distance graph has the shape *K-fabric plus a wall-breaking
rotation*. De Grey's construction, Heule's shrinkings of it, and Haugland's heptagonal variant all
proceed by (i) exhibiting fabric in which every 4-colouring has a monochromatic pair at distance 2 —
inside K this is not merely possible but forced, since the residue colouring makes *every*
distance-2 pair monochromatic — and (ii) killing each such placement with a spindle at distance 2,
whose rotation adjoins √15 ∼ √−1 2-adically. Step (ii) is where K is left, and Theorem B says it
must be: no arrangement of step-(i) material alone can force five colours. In the same light, the
programme's observation that a 4-colourable K-point graph of ~1,800 points becomes 5-chromatic when
united with one copy of itself rotated by the distance-2 spindle angle about almost any vertex, while
copies rotated by the Moser angle, the distance-5 angle, or reflected in a line through two vertices
never do, is the wall seen from inside: those three transformations are defined over K.

## 7. Consequences for the two-distance descent

### 7.1 The programme in mathematical terms

Exoo and Ismailescu (2019) exhibit a 214-point set H ⊂ K², with 1,004 pairs at distance 1 and 446
pairs at distance 2, such that in every proper 5-colouring of the graph with both kinds of edges, two
named points A, B at distance 5 receive the same colour. Rotating H about A by τ₅ (which is defined
over K) produces a 426-point {1, 2}-distance graph in K² with no proper 5-colouring: five colours
cannot avoid both distance 1 and distance 2 in the plane.

The *descent* attacks χ(ℝ²) ≥ 6 by trading the distance-2 edges away. It repeatedly deletes some of
them and adjoins new points at unit distance from two existing points (the two intersections of two
unit circles), chosen so that the compulsion "A and B share a colour in every proper 5-colouring"
survives with fewer distance-2 edges. Two machine-checked lemmas drive it:

* *Spindle theorem.* If every proper k-colouring of a finite graph drawn in the plane with edges of
  length 1 gives two vertices at distance d ≥ 1/2 the same colour, then the union of the graph with
  its copy rotated about one of the two vertices by the angle with cos θ = 1 − 1/(2d²) is not
  k-colourable. If the graph has only unit edges, the union is a unit-distance graph.
* *Spare-colour lemma.* A k-colourable graph carries no pair compulsion at k + 1 colours: given a
  k-colouring, recolour one endpoint with the (k+1)-st colour to separate a pair forced equal, or
  both endpoints (if non-adjacent) to equate a pair forced distinct.

So if the descent ever reaches zero distance-2 edges, the unit-distance graph forces A ≡ B at five
colours, the τ₅-spindle gives a 6-chromatic unit-distance graph, and χ(ℝ²) ≥ 6. At the time of
writing, four rounds of the descent (plus a pruning pass) have reduced the 446 distance-2 edges to
149, on a graph of 1,981 points with 13,796 unit edges — and **every point ever adjoined lies in
K²**, because every point kept so far was the intersection of unit circles about two points whose
distance d satisfied 4/d² − 1 ∈ K×² (§7.3).

### 7.2 What Theorem B says about it

*Theorem 7.1.* No finite unit-distance graph with vertices in K² forces two vertices to share a
colour, or forces two non-adjacent vertices to differ, in every proper 5-colouring.

*Proof.* Theorem B and the spare-colour lemma with k = 4. ∎

So a {1, 2}-distance graph Γ ⊂ K² forcing A ≡ B cannot be a unit-distance graph: **the descent cannot
terminate while its points stay in K².** The residue colouring says more than that, because it is a
single explicit 5-colouring skeleton that the cheats must defeat.

*Lemma 7.2 (no unit chord across a fork of length 2 in K).* If q, p, s ∈ K² with |p − q| = |s − q| = d
and |p − s| = 1, then (s − q)/(p − q) = (2d² − 1 ± i√(4d² − 1))/(2d²) lies in K(i), so √(4d² − 1) ∈ K.
For d = 2 this would put √15 in K; hence two points of K² at distance 2 from a common point of K² are
never one unit apart. (Proof: the ratio has modulus 1 and, by the cosine rule, real part (2d² − 1)/(2d²).)

*The transversal system.* Let Γ = (V, E₁, C) be a K-point graph with unit edges E₁, distance-2 edges
("cheats") C, and a distinguished pair A, B at distance 5. For C′ ⊆ C let Σ(C′) be the 2-SAT instance
with a Boolean x_v for each vertex and the clauses

    x_u ⊕ x_v for every cheat {u, v} ∈ C′;   ¬(x_u ∧ x_v) for every unit edge {u, v} ∈ E₁;   ¬(x_A ∧ x_B).

*Theorem 7.3 (the residue obstruction).* If Σ(C′) is satisfiable, then for EVERY finite H ⊂ K² the
graph on V ∪ H with its unit edges and the cheats C′ has a proper 5-colouring separating A from B.
Consequently no set of K-points, of any size or depth, turns C′ into a forcing set; every standing set
reachable by a descent confined to K² has Σ unsatisfiable.

*Proof.* Let x satisfy Σ(C′) and S = {v : x_v = 1}. Colour S with a fifth colour and everything else
(V ∖ S and all of H) by the residue colouring r of §5.3, translated to the component of A. Unit edges
inside V ∖ S ∪ H are proper because r is; unit edges within S are excluded by the second clause type;
an edge between S and its complement sees the fifth colour against a residue. Every cheat has exactly
one endpoint in S, so it sees the fifth colour against a residue. A and B are not both in S, and if
neither is, r(A) ≠ r(B) because |A − B| = 5 has 𝔭-adic valuation 0. ∎

Because the XOR clauses alone are satisfiable iff the cheat graph (V, C′) is bipartite, a standing set
the in-field descent can reach either contains an **odd cycle of distance-2 pairs** or has every choice
of bipartition sides blocked by unit adjacencies or by {A, B}.

*Corollary 7.4 (three is the floor inside K).* A standing set reachable inside K² has at least three
cheats; with exactly three, the cheats form a distance-2 triangle or a configuration in which all four
transversals are blocked by unit edges.

*Proof.* Zero cheats: S = ∅ satisfies Σ. One cheat {p, q}: S = {p}. Two cheats sharing a vertex p:
S = {p}. Two disjoint cheats {p, q}, {p′, q′}: the four transversals are {x, y} with x ∈ {p, q},
y ∈ {p′, q′}. If all four were unit-adjacent, p′ and q′ would both be at unit distance from both ends
of the length-2 segment pq, hence both its midpoint, contradicting |p′ − q′| = 2. So some transversal
is independent, and it fails only if it is {A, B}, say A = p and B = p′; but then the other three are
blocked by adjacency, which gives |A − B| ≤ |p − q′| + |q′ − p′| = 3 < 5. Three cheats: a star at q is
satisfied by S = {q}; a path p–q–s–t by S = {p, s} (p and s are both at distance 2 from q, so not
adjacent by Lemma 7.2, and |p − s| ≤ 4 < 5 so {p, s} ≠ {A, B}); a path p–q–s plus a disjoint cheat
{p′, q′} has the four transversals {q, p′}, {q, q′}, {p, s, p′}, {p, s, q′}, all of which are blocked
only in special position — for instance p, p′, q, q′, s collinear at unit spacing, where every
transversal contains a unit edge; a triangle is an odd cycle. ∎

The obstruction is exactly what the descent must overcome to leave the residue colouring behind, and
it is decidable in milliseconds for any candidate batch: a 2-SAT instance on the seed vertices, with
no geometry beyond the cheat graph.

### 7.3 Which new points can break the wall

*Lemma 7.5 (one gluing step).* Let a, b ∈ F² have distance d with 0 < d < 2. The two points at unit
distance from both are

    p± = (a + b)/2 ± v·√(4/d² − 1),   v = i(b − a)/2 ∈ F² \ {0},

so p± ∈ F(√r)² with r = 4/d² − 1 ∈ F, and p± ∈ F² iff r ∈ F×². Consequently the set of all points at
unit distance from two points of a finite S ⊂ F² lies in F(√r₁, …, √rₘ) for the finitely many
radicands rₖ = 4/dₖ² − 1 over pairs of S, and only square classes of such rₖ can be adjoined in one
step. Iterating from a K-point set produces only multiquadratic extensions of K (never, for instance,
Haugland's cubic field).

*Proof.* The perpendicular bisector of ab meets the unit circles about a and b where the height h
satisfies h² = 1 − d²/4, so p± = midpoint ± (unit normal)·h, and (unit normal)·h = v·(2h/d) =
v·√(4/d² − 1). If √r ∉ F then p± ∉ F² (else √r = (p± − midpoint)/v ∈ F). ∎

For K the rational square classes reachable in one step were measured on the current object to be
{1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 14, 35, 70}, together with many non-rational classes (radicands such as 1 + √3). By
§6.2 only the classes 5 and 7 (mod 8) can break the wall among rational radicands: √5 is adjoined by
a pair at d² = 4/(1 + 5q²) for some q ∈ K (for instance d² = 2/3), √7 by a pair at d² = 4/(1 + 7q²)
(for instance d = 1/√2), while √2, √10, √14, √35, √70 are provably useless on their own (though
combinations may not be: K(√2, √14) ∋ √7). For a general radicand r ∈ K the test is local: writing
r₁, r₂ for the images of r under the two 2-adic embeddings K ↪ ℚ₂(√3), adjoining √r removes the
conjugation-fixed prime above 2 iff −r₁ and −r₂ are both squares in ℚ₂(√3). Since a 𝔭-adic unit of
ℚ₂(√3) is a square iff it is a square modulo a fixed power of the maximal ideal, this is a finite
congruence check, exact and cheap, that can be applied to every candidate point before any search.

The reading for the programme is therefore: restricting the construction to K-points, as the
current round does, can lower the count of distance-2 edges but can never finish; at least one
wall-breaking adjunction (a distance-2 spindle copy being the classical one) is mandatory, and the
useful out-of-field material is precisely characterised by the 2-adic test above.

### 7.4 The programme's own object, measured against the theory

Everything above was tested on the descent's current object (1,981 points of which 186 are live seed
vertices, 13,796 unit edges, 149 standing cheats after the pruning pass) and on its most recent
replay, before any of it was believed.

* **The residue obstruction is consistent with every certificate.** The standing cheat graph has 24
  components, 8 of them non-bipartite (sizes 13, 12, 11, 9, 9, 9, 8, 8), and its shortest odd cycle is
  a distance-2 triangle; Σ is unsatisfiable, as the forcing certificate requires. Removing any single
  cheat leaves Σ unsatisfiable (0 of 149 cheats are residue-blocked), and so does removing the two
  cheats of the last batch. The seed's 446 cheats form 16 components, all non-bipartite.
* **No in-field survivor is residue-type.** Of the 81,787 separating colourings enumerated for that
  two-cheat batch, 77,851 were killed by in-field material and 3,936 survived; the theory predicts
  survivors of residue type only when Σ becomes satisfiable, and finds none among the 3,936 (checked
  against both residue colourings).
* **The wall-breaking material is thin, and structured.** Over the 950,371 pairs of object points at
  distance below 2 there are 1,435 distinct radicands 4/d² − 1. Thirty-two are squares in K (140,195
  pairs — the in-field material). Exactly **five** break the wall alone (4,221 pairs, 0.44 %): the
  √5-class (4,092 pairs, at distances 4/3 and 2√5/3) and three others carrying 129 pairs. A further 205
  radicands (81,592 pairs) break it at one 2-adic prime only and need a partner. And 523,608 pairs —
  two thirds of the out-of-field material — have radicands that are squares in ℚ₂(√3) at both primes:
  adjoining them can never help, in any combination. All of this has one explanation: every point of
  the object has the Exoo–Ismailescu shape (x ∈ √3·ℚ(√33), y ∈ ℚ(√33)), so every squared distance and
  every radicand lies in ℚ(√33), whose two 2-adic images are rational; the square classes of ℚ₂ in
  ℚ₂(√3) form the group ⟨−1, 2⟩ of order four, and the census finds exactly the 16 class pairs of that
  group, with the wall broken iff −1 is generated at both primes.

* **Single wall-breaking points kill nothing.** Exported as a pool of their own, the 8,442 glue points
  of the wall-breaking radicands (each with exactly two unit neighbours in the object, 34,518 unit
  edges among themselves) were replayed against the 3,936 in-field survivors: every one of the 3,936
  colourings extends, in 19 ms median — 0 kills. Breaking the wall is necessary for the endgame; a
  handful of degree-two points is not what does it. The 572,992-point pool of every radicand with a
  nontrivial 2-adic class, sampled at 394 of the survivors with five-minute walls, returned 394
  undecided, 0 killed, 0 provably surviving: at that size the ask is beyond a five-minute solver
  budget, and whether combined wall-breaking material kills at five colours is open.

The reading for the programme is now quantitative: the descent confined to K cannot end (Theorem 7.1),
can go no lower than three cheats (Corollary 7.4), and is currently far from that floor (eight odd
components); the material that can take it past the wall is a few thousand pairs of the √5-class, or
combinations of the "one-prime" classes, and nothing else at depth one.

### 7.5 A witness the theory predicted

The rigidity measurement of §8.7 says the programme's K-point fabric forces 163 of its 178 pairs at
distance 8/3 to share a colour in every proper 4-colouring. The spindle theorem (§7.1) turns any such
pair into a 5-chromatic unit-distance graph: rotate the fabric about one endpoint x by the angle
with cos θ = 1 − 1/(2d²) = 119/128, sin θ = 3√247/128, so that the other endpoint y lands one unit
from its image, and the union cannot be 4-coloured. Theorem B demands that the union leave every
field where the wall stands, and it does: 247 = 13·19 ≡ 7 (mod 8), so √247 makes −1 a 2-adic square.
Built for one pair: 3,905 points, 27,593 unit edges, 8 cross chords (found at 60 digits, no near
miss), refusing four colours by a direct SAT ask and admitting five. Its coordinates lie in
ℚ(√3, √11, √247) — a 5-chromatic unit-distance graph in a field containing neither √5 nor √7, which
every published witness needs (§6.3). The same construction at the 8/√3 pairs gives ℚ(√3, √11, √23).
The forcing core of a single pair, shrunk to 1-minimality by greedy outside-in deletion, has 588
vertices and 2,886 unit edges, every vertex necessary; core ∪ copy is a 1,175-point 5-chromatic
unit-distance graph with 5,773 unit edges and 6 cross chords, again refused at four colours directly.
For comparison, de Grey's forcer of a pair at distance 4 has 791 vertices and needs √5 and √7.

## 8. What is open after this

The registered conjecture is settled in the affirmative. What it leaves — and what its proof
suggests — is the following.

1. **The next field up.** Heule's 510-vertex graph lies in ℚ(√3, √5, √11)², so that plane needs
   at least five colours. Is its chromatic number exactly 5? Residue colourings cannot decide this:
   their bounds are powers of 2 or perfect squares, and the best they give here is 121 (the
   conjugation-fixed prime above 11). A proof of χ(G(ℚ(√3, √5, √11)²)) = 5 would need a genuinely
   new colouring idea; a 6-chromatic unit-distance graph in that plane would solve Hadwiger–Nelson's
   lower bound.
2. **√7 without √5.** The wall also falls for ℚ(√3, √7, √11) (7 ≡ 7 mod 8), but no 5-chromatic
   unit-distance graph is known there — de Grey's uses both √5 and √7. Is χ(G(ℚ(√3, √7, √11)²)) ≥ 5?
3. **Triangle-free planes.** For a quadratic field ℚ(√d), the wall falls only when d ≡ 7 (mod 8)
   (d = 7, 15, 23, 31, …). These planes contain no equilateral triangle. What are their chromatic
   numbers? Any 5-chromatic unit-distance graph in one of them would be triangle-free and would live
   in a quadratic field — the smallest conceivable coordinate field for such a graph.
4. **Exactness of the residue bound.** With Theorem A′, ℚ(√3) and ℚ(√7) are settled at 3. For ℚ(√5)
   and ℚ(√11) the invariant gives 4 and the truth is 2, 3 or 4 (neither has triangles; ℚ(√11)²
   contains the Moser twist ρ = (5 + i√11)/6 as a unit vector). Are either of these 4-chromatic?
5. **Additive versus arbitrary colourings.** The colouring of Theorem B is additive in Fischer's
   sense. Where the residue method fails, could a non-additive colouring still succeed? For
   ℚ(√3, √5, √11) Heule's graph says no at four colours. Whether Theorem B itself, or the
   criterion of §6, already appears in Fischer (1990) or its successors has not been checked; the
   ingredients are classical and it would not be surprising.
7. **Rigidity of the residue colouring.** Are the two residue colourings the only proper 4-colourings
   of G(K²), up to relabelling and the choice of coset representatives? For ℚ² the analogous statement
   is true (a bipartite graph has one 2-colouring per component). Finite K-point graphs certainly have
   other 4-colourings — on the descent's 1,953-point object a complete sweep finds no forced
   monochromatic pair among the 15,230 pairs at the valuation-2 and valuation-4 distances (2, 4,
   2√3, 2/√3, 4/√3), but 163 of the 178 pairs at 8/3 and 8 of the 11 at 8/√3, the two classes of
   valuation 6, are forced equal in every proper 4-colouring; one growth step earlier the counts were
   131 and 6. Rigidity toward the residue colouring appears to arrive from the deepest valuations
   downward, and the question proper is about the infinite graph, where boundary freedom
   disappears. A positive answer would explain the distance-2 pigeonhole (every 4-colouring of deep
   fabric has a monochromatic distance-2 pair) as the shadow of uniqueness.
8. **The 2-adic classes of a lattice.** The descent's object realises only the four square classes
   ⟨−1, 2⟩ at each 2-adic prime because its points keep the Exoo–Ismailescu shape. Which classes a
   general K-point construction can reach, and how many gluing steps it takes to leave ℚ(√33), is a
   question about the lattice, not the field.
6. **Machine verification.** Theorem B rests on Lemma 5.2 (the splitting of 2 in ℚ(i, √3, √11) and
   the fact that conjugation fixes the primes above it) and on the valuation identity in Theorem A.
   Both are formalisable; the programme's existing library (unit-distance graphs over ℂ with
   coordinates in a multiquadratic tower, the spare-colour lemma, the spindle theorem) supplies the
   graph-theoretic side. Until that is done, this note's proof has been checked by hand only.

## 9. How to check this note

Everything reduces to a few verifiable facts.

1. *Lemma 4.1 and the spindle coordinates (§4):* rational arithmetic in the basis 1, √3, √11, √33.
2. *Lemma 5.2:* (i) 33 ≡ 1 (mod 8), so 33 is a 2-adic square; (ii) x² + x + 1 is irreducible over
   𝔽₂, so ℚ₂(ζ₃) is the unramified quadratic extension; (iii) ℚ₂(i)/ℚ₂ is ramified; (iv) the seven
   quadratic subfields of ℚ(i, √3, √11) are ℚ(√d), d ∈ {−1, ±3, ±11, ±33}, and only 33 ≡ 1 (mod 8);
   (v) complex conjugation fixes √33.
3. *Theorem A:* for a unit vector u, v_𝔭(u) + v_𝔭(ū) = 0 and v_𝔭(ū) = v_{𝔭̄}(u); with 𝔭̄ = 𝔭 this
   forces v_𝔭(u) = 0; the residue map is additive.
4. *A finite, decisive test.* Take the 214 Exoo–Ismailescu points ((a√3 + b√11)/12, (c + d√33)/12).
   Fix a base vertex z₀ and colour each vertex z of its component by r(z − z₀), computing residues in
   ℚ₂(ζ₁₂) to modest 2-adic precision by the recipe of §5.3 (√33 ↦ s with s ≡ 17 mod 256; √3 ↦
   −i(2ζ₃ + 1); reduce a + b·i to (a + b) mod 2 ∈ 𝔽₄). The prediction is sharp: the colouring must be
   proper on all 1,004 unit edges and must give the same colour to both endpoints of every one of the
   446 distance-2 edges. Either failure would refute the argument; both successes on a graph that
   provably has no proper 5-colouring separating A from B are strong evidence that nothing has been
   missed.

*Provenance.* Sections 1–4 and 7.1 restate a question raised inside a computational programme on
the Hadwiger–Nelson problem; the machine-checked results referred to (the 4-chromatic seam lattices,
the distance-2 pigeonhole, the spindle theorem, the spare-colour lemma, and the exact coordinate
fields of the de Grey and Heule graphs) are that programme's. The proof in §5 and the criterion in
§6 were found on 2026-09-05 while writing this note; the residue obstruction of §7.2 and the
measurements of §7.4 were added the same day. None of it has yet been checked by anyone else, but the
colouring was validated by computation on the 214-point seed and on the programme's 1,981-point object
(proper on every unit edge, monochromatic on every distance-2 pair, the forced pair separated).
