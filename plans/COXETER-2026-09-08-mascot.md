# The mascot of `ℚ(√3, √7, √11)` — derivation, prior art, and the one open step

*Coxeter, 2026-09-08. Written against the mascot challenge. Everything below is derivation or
citation; nothing was found by search. The one computational step is an exhaustive check of a
16-vertex graph's colourings, used to verify a claim already derived.*

## 0. Summary

1. **A rigidity theorem** (§2, new as far as I can find): a proper 4-colouring of the K-fabric with
   no monochromatic `√3`-**pair** is *exactly* one of Theorem B's two residue colourings. Three
   steps: a hexagon lemma, `Σ₀/2Σ₀ ≅ 𝔽₄ × 𝔽₄`, and the fact that `K₄ × K₄` has only projections.
2. **The consequence** (§3): in every proper 4-colouring of the K-fabric, *either* some `√3`-pair is
   monochromatic, *or* **every** pair at 2-adically even distance is monochromatic — 2, 4, 8/3,
   8/√3 all at once. This explains, in one line, the whole of Parts's published table of which
   distances admit mono-pair and non-mono-pair gadgets (§4).
3. **The prior art is much larger than the record shows** (§4), and it changes the target.
4. **The target** (§5): Parts's human-verifiable proof reaches `χ ≥ 5` with a final witness in
   `ℚ(√3, √5, √7, √11)`. Its `√5` enters at exactly **one** step — a phase-pinning rotation by
   `arccos(7/8)`. Replacing that rotation by the **Moser twist `arccos(5/6)`**, which is native to
   `K`, would put the witness in **`ℚ(√3, √7, √11)` — a 6-open field, no `√5`**. That is the
   mascot, and the pinning lemma is the one open step.

## 1. Setting

`K = ℚ(√3, √11)`; `Σ₀ ⊂ K(i) = ℂ` is the group generated by the unit vectors, i.e. the connected
component of `0` in the unit-distance graph. `Σ₀` is a module over `R = ℤ[norm-one elements of
K(i)]`, and Theorem A is the statement that `R` lies in the valuation ring at each of the two primes
`𝔭₁, 𝔭₂` above 2. `R ⊇ ℤ[1/3][ζ₆, ρ]` with `ρ = (5 + i√11)/6` the Moser twist; `Σ₀ = R`.

## 2. The rigidity theorem

**Hexagon lemma.** Let `O` be a point and `O + ζ₆^k u` (`k = 0..5`) a hexagonal shell of unit
neighbours. If no two shell points at distance `√3` share a colour, then the rim is a proper
3-colouring of `C₆` determined by one alternating triple, and **all three diameters are
monochromatic**: `c(O + 2u') = c(O)` for the three `u'` in the shell.

*Proof.* The rim avoids `c(O)`, so uses 3 colours. Positions `0, 2, 4` are pairwise at `√3`, hence
rainbow, hence all three colours. Position 1 differs from 0 and 2, so equals position 4. Likewise
`3 = 0` and `5 = 2`. Positions `k` and `k+3` are at distance 2. ∎ *(6 clean rim colourings,
0 violating, verified exhaustively.)*

**Corollary.** If a 4-colouring of `Σ₀` has no monochromatic `√3`-pair, then `c(z) = c(z + 2u)` for
every `z` and every unit `u`, so `c` factors through `Σ₀/2Σ₀`.

**The quotient.** `ζ₆² = ζ₆ − 1` and `ρ² = (5/3)ρ − 1`; modulo 2, with `3` invertible and `3 ≡ 1`,
both become `x² + x + 1 = 0`. Hence `R/2R = 𝔽₄ ⊗_{𝔽₂} 𝔽₄ ≅ 𝔽₄ × 𝔽₄`, the two projections being
reduction at `𝔭₁` and at `𝔭₂`. The units `ζ₆^a ρ^b` map to `(ζ̄^{a+b}, ζ̄^{a+2b})`, and `(a,b) ↦
(a+b, a+2b)` is invertible mod 3, so **the unit vectors surject onto `𝔽₄* × 𝔽₄*`** (verified: 9 of 9).

**The quotient graph is `K₄ × K₄`** — vertices `𝔽₄ × 𝔽₄`, adjacent iff *both* coordinates differ.

**Theorem.** *Every proper 4-colouring of the K-fabric with no monochromatic `√3`-pair is one of
the two residue colourings of Theorem B, up to relabelling.*

*Proof.* By the corollary it factors through `K₄ × K₄`; that graph has exactly 48 proper
4-colourings and every one is a projection onto a coordinate (exhaustive check, 16 vertices), i.e.
reduction at `𝔭₁` or at `𝔭₂` composed with a bijection. ∎

The `K₄ × K₄` step is the Greenwell–Lovász phenomenon (`K_n` is uniquely `n`-colourable in the
categorical product); it is classical and no novelty is claimed for it. What is new is that the
quotient of the K-fabric by its own doubling *is* `K₄ × K₄`, with the two factors being the two
primes above 2 — the combinatorial rigidity and the arithmetic are literally the same object.

## 3. The dichotomy, and what it says about distances

**Corollary.** In every proper 4-colouring of the K-fabric, either some `√3`-pair is monochromatic,
or every pair whose difference lies in `2Σ₀` is monochromatic.

The second alternative is not a weak statement: `2Σ₀` contains `2`, `4`, `8/3`, `8/√3`, `2√3`,
`4/3`, `4/√3`, `2/√3` — every distance the handoff's spindle table lists. So on the clean stratum
*all* the candidate forced pairs are forced at once, and the entire content of a forcing gadget is
the exclusion of monochromatic `√3`-pairs.

**Necessity, in the other direction.** The residue colouring separates every pair whose difference
has `𝔭`-valuation 0. So over `K` a forced-equal pair must have 2-adically even distance and a
forced-distinct pair 2-adically odd distance. This is not new here (M60 has the finite-plane face of
it), but combined with §4 it acquires a use.

## 5. The target: `ℚ(√3, √7, √11)`

Parts's proof runs: **(a)** every equilateral `√3`-triple is non-mono (the computer part);
**(b)** a wheel with two non-mono `√3`-triples has two colourings, so a hexagonal lattice is, in at
least one of its three directions, a stack of **alternating two-coloured lattice lines**;
**(c)** doubling the wheel gives a 7-point set using two colours, three ways; **(d)** two such sets
at a common centre, **one rotated by `arccos(7/8)`**, have two colourings, and *any pair at distance
4 is monochromatic*; **(e)** spindle at `arccos(31/32)`.

Two observations.

**The striped family of (b) is the residue family with a free phase.** Write `z = m + nζ₆`. A
striped colouring is `c(m + nζ₆) = P_{n mod 2}[(m + φ_n) mod 2]` for a partition of the four colours
into two pairs `P₀, P₁` and an arbitrary phase sequence `φ_n ∈ {0,1}`; the pairs must alternate by
row parity because each point has *both* `m`-parities among its neighbours in each adjacent row.
Setting all `φ_n` equal within each parity class gives exactly reduction mod `2ℤ[ζ₆]`. And a
monochromatic `√3`-pair occurs precisely at a **phase disagreement** between rows `n` and `n−2`
(difference `1 − 2ζ₆`, norm 3). So §2's theorem and Parts's step (b) are the same statement seen
from the two sides: no mono `√3`-pair ⟺ no phase disagreement ⟺ residue.

**`√5` enters at exactly one step.** Steps (a), (b), (c) are inside `K`. Step (e) adjoins
`√(4·16 − 1) = √63 = 3√7`. Only step (d) adjoins `√15 ∼ √5`, and its job is to **pin the phases**
so that distance-4 pairs are monochromatic in *every* direction, not only along the stripes.
`arccos(7/8)` is the rotation that brings the radius-2 shells of two copies of `ℤ[ζ₆]` to unit
distance.

**The mascot, therefore.** The Moser twist `ρ = (5 + i√11)/6`, `arccos(5/6)`, brings the
**radius-`√3` shells** of two copies of `ℤ[ζ₆]` to unit distance — the norm-3 elements are lattice
points, so this is as natural as Parts's choice, and it is native to `K`. If two hexagonal lattices
sharing a centre, one rotated by the Moser twist and joined by the unit edges between their `√3`
shells, pin the phases the way Parts's `arccos(7/8)` pair does, then

> every pair at distance 4 is monochromatic in every proper 4-colouring of a **`K`-object**,
> and the `arccos(31/32)` spindle gives a 5-chromatic unit-distance graph in
> **`ℚ(√3, √7, √11)`** — degree 8, no `√5`, and **6-open** by M58's sieve.

That would answer `plans/FIELD-WALL-THEOREM.md` §8 item 2 ("`√7` without `√5`"), improve the field of
the best published human-verifiable proof, and land the only one of the challenge's four candidate
fields in which a 6-chromatic graph can exist at all.

**The open step, stated precisely.** Let `L₀ = ℤ[ζ₆]` and `L₁ = ρL₀`, and let `c` be a proper
4-colouring of `L₀ ∪ L₁` with no monochromatic `√3`-triple. By (b) each `L_i` is striped in some
direction with some phase sequence. Does the cross-edge system between the `√3` shells force the
phases constant — equivalently, force `c` to be a residue colouring on `L₀ ∪ L₁`? A finite,
hand-checkable question about two hexagonal lattices and one rotation.

## 6. Honest verdict on the challenge as posed

* "Derive the mascot of a 5-chromatic field one rung up" is the right question with the wrong field
  list. Of the four named, `K(√5)`, `K(√23)`, `K(√247)` are 5-sealed by M58 and a mascot for any of
  them has provably no bearing on `χ ≥ 6`; `K(√7)` is the live one, and the framing excludes it
  because its 5-chromaticity is not yet known — which is precisely what makes it worth deriving.
* The `8/3` mascot in `K(√247)` is prior art: the fan of four rhombi is Parts §2, the mono-pair at
  `8/3` is his Table 1 at 367 vertices, and the field is Exoo–Ismailescu's.
* No fully hand-derived 5-chromatic graph exists, here or in the literature. Parts's is
  hand-*verifiable* and its hard lemma is still computer-found. The rigidity theorem of §2 does not
  change that; what it changes is that the whole difficulty is now provably concentrated in one
  place — excluding monochromatic `√3`-pairs — and everything downstream of that exclusion is
  arithmetic.

## 7. The cross-edge theorem, and what it settles about §5

Derived after §5 was written, and it **refutes §5's first proposal while sharpening the target.**

**Theorem (cross edges of a twisted lattice pair).** Let `d > 0` with `d²` a Loeschian integer, let
`ρ_d = (2d² − 1 + i√(4d² − 1))/(2d²)`, and let `L₀ = ℤ[ζ₆]`, `L₁ = ρ_d L₀`. Then the unit-distance
edges between `L₀` and `L₁` are **exactly the six pairs `(w, ρ_d w)` with `|w| = d`**, together with
the star of the shared centre `0`.

*Proof.* `|z − ρ_d w|² = |z|² + |w|² − 2Re(z̄ ρ_d w)`. Write `z̄w = a + bζ₆` with `a, b ∈ ℤ`; then
`Re(z̄ρ_d w) = a·Re ρ_d + (b/2)(Re ρ_d) − (b√3/2)(Im ρ_d)`, and `√3·Im ρ_d` is irrational over `ℚ`
in every case (`√33/12` for the Moser twist, `3√5/16` for `arccos(7/8)`, `3√21/64` for
`arccos(31/32)`), so `b = 0`: `w` is a **rational multiple `t` of `z`**. With `N = |z|²` and
`r = Re ρ_d = 1 − 1/(2d²)`, the edge condition becomes `N(t² − 2rt + 1) = 1`. Solving,
`t = r ± √(r² − 1 + 1/N)`, so `N ≤ 1/(1 − r²)`; the finitely many Loeschian `N` in range are checked
one at a time, and in every case the only solution with `tz ∈ ℤ[ζ₆]` is `t = 1`, `N = d²`. ∎

*(Checked at `d = √3, 2, 4, 2√3`: in each case exactly one admissible `(N, t) = (d², 1)`.)*

**Consequence 1 — Parts's pinning gadget is canonical, and it is 13 points.** Centre, plus two
radius-`d` hexagons, plus six cross edges. His `arccos(7/8)` object is the case `d = 2`.

**Consequence 2 — the Moser twist cannot pin, and §5's proposal is dead.** The pinning step needs the
radius-`d` hexagon to collapse to **two** colours in a striped colouring, and by §5's description a
striped colouring is constant-per-`(m mod 2, row)`, so the hexagon collapses **iff its points lie in
`2ℤ[ζ₆]`, i.e. iff `d²` is a Loeschian multiple of 4**. The Moser twist's shell has `d² = 3` — odd —
and its six points straddle both row parities, so it uses colours from *both* pairs and pins nothing.
**`√3` is the wrong shell for exactly the reason `√3` is the wrong distance everywhere in this
theory: it is 2-adically odd.** The pinning rotation is forced to be `ρ_d` with `4 | d²`, and the
smallest is `d = 2`, whose `ρ` adjoins `√15 ∼ √5`. **So the `√5` in Parts's human-verifiable proof
is not a convenience — it is forced by the smallest admissible pinning shell.**

**Consequence 3 — the honest remaining route to `K(√7)`, and its open point.** The next admissible
shells are `d² = 12` (`ρ` adjoins `√47`, and `K(√47)` is 5-sealed) and `d² = 16` (`ρ = arccos(31/32)`
adjoins `√63 = 3√7`). Pinning at `d = 4` therefore stays inside **`K(√7)`**, and — this is where §2
earns its keep — *if* the pinning forces full phase constancy, §2's theorem upgrades it to "every
pair at 2-adically even distance is monochromatic", including distance 4, whose spindle is
`arccos(31/32)` again. Same rotation twice; the whole object lands in `ℚ(√3, √7, √11)`, no `√5`.

**The open point, stated so it can be attacked or refuted.** The `d = 2` gadget pins `φ_n = φ_{n±2}`
and, translated over the lattice, gives phase constancy outright. The `d = 4` gadget pins only
`φ_n = φ_{n±4}` — **phase constancy modulo 4, not constancy** — because its hexagon sits in rows
`0, ±4`. Closing the gap needs either a second family of constraints tying `φ_n` to `φ_{n+2}` inside
`K(√7)`, or the three stripe directions playing against each other. I have not closed it, and I am
reporting it as not closed.

> A distance-4 pair is forced over K-fabric **iff every 4-colouring that has a monochromatic

**Both boundaries of §2's hypothesis, stated so the theorem is not read as more than it is.**

## 9. The conditional/unconditional split, and what §2 is when read as a conditional forcer

Poros's reading of Exoo–Ismailescu's `G40` — a **conditional** forced pair, forty points against our
588 for the unconditional one at the same distance in the same field — reframes §2, and the reframing
is the most useful thing in this file.

**§2 *is* a conditional forced pair, at every 2-adically even distance simultaneously, on zero extra
points.** Its condition is "no monochromatic `√3`-pair"; its conclusion is the residue colouring,
which monochromes `2`, `4`, `8/3`, `8/√3`, … at once and **in every direction**. Exoo–Ismailescu paid
forty points for one distance under one condition. The hexagonal fabric pays nothing for all of them
under a different one.

**Discharging it by their move — spindling — gives a theorem.** Let `ρ₄ = (31 + 3i√7)/32`, the
spindle rotation at distance 4, and let `Σ₀` be the K-fabric.

> **Theorem.** Every proper 4-colouring of `Σ₀ ∪ ρ₄Σ₀` has a monochromatic `√3`-pair inside one of
> the two copies.

*Proof.* Suppose not. Each copy is an isometric image of `Σ₀` with no monochromatic `√3`-pair, so by
§2 each is residue-coloured in its own transported arithmetic. `4 ∈ 2Σ₀`, so `c(0) = c(4)` in the
first copy and `c(0) = c(4ρ₄)` in the second. And `|1 − ρ₄|² = 2 − 2·(31/32) = 1/16`, so
`|4 − 4ρ₄| = 1`: two adjacent points, both coloured `c(0)`. ∎

Two lines, no search, and the object is a `K(√7)`-object — the same field the §7 route targets,
reached from the other side. By compactness a finite subgraph suffices, which is the finite carrier
this statement is worth having for.

**What it is not.** A pigeonhole, not a contradiction — the exact shape of Exoo–Ismailescu's
Claim 2.1, and it fails to close for the exact reason theirs does: killing a monochromatic `√3`-pair
is not something anyone knows how to do (Parts's Table 1 has non-mono-pair gadgets at `3`, `7/3`,
`5/3`, `1/3`, `√(11/3)` and **not** at `√3`, which is why de Grey and Parts both work with non-mono
`√3`-*triples* instead).

**Where that leaves the route, honestly.** Under Parts's discharged lemma — no monochromatic
`√3`-*triple*, his 481-vertex computer part — each lattice copy is striped, and a striped colouring
splits into (i) no phase disagreement, hence residue, hence every even pair monochromatic in every
direction; or (ii) a phase disagreement, which *is* a monochromatic `√3`-pair, at a locatable place.
Case (i) on both copies fires the spindle above and contradicts. **So the entire remaining problem is
case (ii): a striped colouring with a phase disagreement.** That is a far smaller target than "an
arbitrary 4-colouring", it is stated in coordinates, and it is where I would spend next. Not closed,
and reported as not closed.

`√3`-pigeonhole" — the latter would be discounted on sight by any reader who knows M34, and rightly.

**Two corrections to the paragraph above, both Poros's, and then the check that settles it.**

*First, the defence was resting on the wrong sentence.* `core9_plane` reads
`(C : (unitDistance ℂ).Coloring (Fin 4)) : ∃ x y, dist x y = √3 ∧ C x = C y` — it takes a proper
4-colouring of the **plane** as a hypothesis, and this repository kernel-certifies `χ(ℝ²) ≥ 5`, so no
such `C` exists and **the plane statement is vacuous**. "A subset colouring need not extend" is true
but beside the point; all of the content sits in the finite `core9_pigeonhole`, and that is the only
statement that could subsume §9.

*Second, that makes subsumption decidable*: does the nine-point carrier embed in `Σ₀ ∪ ρ₄Σ₀`?

**It does not, and the obstruction is one line.** Squared distances are isometry invariants, so a
configuration drawable with coordinates in a real field `F` has all its squared distances in `F`.
Computing the carrier's 36 pairwise squared distances exactly in the `ℚ(√3,√5,√7,√11)` tower (the
stored integer coordinates at denominator 512, multiplied out in the 16-dimensional multiquadratic

component** — for instance the pairs `(1,7)`, `(1,8)`, `(2,7)`, `(2,8)`, `(3,5)`, `(3,6)`. Since
`√5 ∉ ℚ(√3,√7,√11)`, **the nine-point carrier cannot be drawn in `K(√7)²` at all**, by any isometry.

**Sharper, on Poros's independent reproduction and confirmed here.** It is not merely that the
squared distances lie in `ℚ(√5)`: across all 36, **the only radicands that occur at all are 1 and 5**
— not one non-zero `√3`, `√7` or `√11` coefficient anywhere in the table (asserted over all 36, not
sampled). The carrier's metric is *exactly* a `ℚ(√5)` object. And the ten `√5`-bearing values form a
conjugation-closed multiset with three rational parts, each occurring with both signs —
`(9 ∓ 3√5)/8` twice each, `(13 ∓ 3√5)/4` twice each, `(23 ∓ 3√5)/8` once each — which is the
configuration's mirror symmetry appearing as Galois conjugation, and a free check that both parses
are right.

**And this is why §9's field is not a technicality.** `K(√5)` on its own is **5-sealed** (5 is a
residue mod 11, so 11 splits and stays usable), so any construction routed through the nine-point

`ℚ(√3,√5,√7,√11)` at degree 16 rather than something smaller. *(The tempting over-generalisation is

So §9 is not subsumed, and §9a is a positive claim rather than a defence:

> The nine-point carrier of the certified `√3`-pigeonhole is intrinsically a `√5`-object — its very

Two caveats kept, so the claim does not drift. §9's carrier is **infinite as stated**; a finite one
exists by compactness but has not been extracted, so nothing is claimed about its size and "smallest

## 11. The one case, named

Not "the problem is reduced". **One case**, in coordinates.

Write `z = m + nζ₆` for the triangular lattice `L = ℤ[ζ₆]`; call `n` the *row*. Under Parts's
discharged lemma (no monochromatic `√3`-triple — his 481-vertex computer part), every proper
4-colouring of `L` is **striped**: there is a partition of the four colours into two pairs `P₀, P₁`
and a phase sequence `φ_n ∈ {0,1}` with

  `c(m + nζ₆) = P_{n mod 2}[(m + φ_n) mod 2]`,

the pairs alternating by row parity because each point has *both* `m`-parities among its neighbours in
each adjacent row. Setting all `φ_n` equal within each parity class is exactly reduction mod `2ℤ[ζ₆]`.

**Case (i) — no phase wall.** Then `c` is the residue colouring; §2 applies, every 2-adically even
pair is monochromatic in every direction, and §9's spindle at `ρ₄` closes it. Done.

**Case (ii) — some `n` has `φ_n ≠ φ_{n+2}`.** The `√3`-vectors joining row `n` to row `n+2` are
`−1 + 2ζ₆` and its reflection (`|−1+2ζ₆|² = 1 − 2 + 4 = 3`), and the two endpoints lie in the *same*
row-parity class, so they wear the same colour pair; they agree exactly when `φ_n ≠ φ_{n+2}`. Hence:

> **A phase wall is not one monochromatic `√3`-pair — it is a whole lattice line of them.** For every
> `m ∈ ℤ` the pair `(m + nζ₆, (m−1) + (n+2)ζ₆)` is monochromatic at distance `√3`: two parallel
> lattice lines, matched by one fixed `√3`-vector, every matched pair monochromatic.

That is the case, and it is a *structure*, not a residue — which is why it is worth naming rather than
bounding. Two remarks that sharpen the target and one that limits it.

* **It is invisible to Parts's lemma, necessarily.** Every `√3`-triple of `L` spans three consecutive
  rows or rows `n, n+1, n+2`, so it always meets both parity classes and can never be monochromatic —
  *no* striped colouring has a monochromatic `√3`-triple, at any phases. So the computer part cannot
  reach case (ii) even in principle, and any argument that closes it must be new.
* **The cross-edge budget is exactly six** (§7): between `L` and `ρ₄L` the unit edges are the six pairs
  at radius 4 and nothing else. Parts closes his analogous case with six such edges at radius 2, on a
  13-point set, because striping has already cut each wheel to two colours. Whether six edges at
  radius 4 can defeat a phase wall — rather than merely pin phases modulo 4, which is all I could get
  in §7 — is the question.
* **Not closed, and I am not going to write that it nearly is.**

### 11a. Why the blindness is the finding, not the caveat (Poros)

**The exhaustion, in two lines.** The `√3`-vectors of `ℤ[ζ₆]` are the six solutions of
`m² + mn + n² = 3`: `±(1,1)`, `±(2,−1)`, `±(1,−2)`. Row parity is `n mod 2`, and only `±(1,−2)` has
even `n`. A `√3`-triangle needs `v`, `w` and `v − w` all of norm 3; with `v = −w = ±(1,−2)` the
difference is `±(2,−4)` of norm 12. **So no `√3`-triangle has all three vertices in one row-parity
class** — verified by exhaustion over all 12 triangles, zero survivors.

**(a) This explains a gap in Parts's Table 1 rather than merely noting it.** The table lists
non-mono-*pair* gadgets at `3`, `7/3`, `5/3`, `1/3`, `√(11/3)` and none at `√3`. Above, that was an
observed absence. It is now a mechanism: de Grey and Parts both route through mono-`√3`-*triples*, no
striped colouring has one at any phases, so **the entire triple-based apparatus is structurally blind
to precisely this class.** An absence with a reason is a different object from an absence — this is
not "here is what is left over" but "here is why the method that produced every known proof could not
have reached case (ii), and therefore where a new ingredient has to go."

**(b) Case (ii) is strictly easier than the problem the literature is stuck on, and saying otherwise
underclaims it.** Calling case (ii) "the whole remaining problem" invites a reader to file it under
*the `√3`-virtual-edge nobody can build*. It is not that problem. The open one is to kill **some**
monochromatic `√3`-pair, somewhere, of unknown location. Case (ii) hands over an **infinite,
translation-invariant family of them, all at one known vector `−1 + 2ζ₆`**, and that difference buys
three things the general problem does not:

1. **One local contradiction suffices** — the wall is translation-invariant along the line, so a
   gadget defeating the pair at any single `m` defeats the wall.
2. **`m` is ours to choose** — place the gadget where the surrounding fabric is most favourable, free,
   and unavailable in the general problem.
3. **Several pairs of the line may be used at once, at known relative positions, inside one gadget** —
   strictly more hypothesis than "one monochromatic pair", and the kind of extra structure that makes
   a six-edge cross budget plausible where it would be hopeless against a single unlocated pair.

So the honest sentence is **"what remains is one case, and it is strictly easier than the case the
literature is stuck on, for three stated reasons"** — not "what remains is the hard open problem".
Overclaiming and underclaiming cost the same thing, which is a reader's correct assessment; §11's
first draft made the second error immediately after this file spent a section warning against the
first.
