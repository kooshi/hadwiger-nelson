Does any finite plane need exactly six colours?

Each of these graphs joins two points of a finite plane when the squared distance between them is exactly 1. Their chromatic numbers run 3, 4, 5, 5 … and then, where they are known again, at least 7. Whether any of them needs exactly six is open at five sizes. Here is what is actually proven, and the one flat quantity that explains the jump.

3 · The numbers

Integers as committed; c computed from them here.

How to read the two ends. A set someone found is a lower bound on α; a certificate is an exact rational proof of an upper bound, re-checkable from the number q alone. The true value lies between, so the flat left edge of the band is a band of lower bounds — it may be measuring how hard people searched rather than the graphs themselves. Only q = 3, 7, 11 have α pinned exactly — and q = 19 is where the exact instrument stops being exact, reading about 6 % above the best known set there against 20 % at 23.

Why the gap between the two ends stays open. The obvious move is an exact solver — these graphs are small, and at q = 19 the graph is 361 points of degree 20. Two were tried on 18 September 2026 and both failed, returning 70 and 66 where a local search finds 76 in seconds. The reason is structural: every reduction rule such a solver owns works by finding a point that differs from its neighbours, and in these planes every point looks the same. And at 19 the graph has no triangles at all, which destroys the bound that prunes the search — it allows 180 where the truth is at most 80. The symmetry that makes the algebraic instruments work is exactly what defeats the combinatorial ones.

Not shown. q = 71, refused six by its exact spectrum, has no recorded search figure to plot. The sixth open case is not a prime-field plane at all but a characteristic-two target of size 4096, which scales differently: its best known set is a subspace.
