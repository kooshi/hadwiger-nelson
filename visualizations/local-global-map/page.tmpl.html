The Local–Global Map

Every row is a field: the points of the plane whose coordinates lie in it. Across the row run its places — the real place, then the primes. Height inside a row is a number of colours, two at the floor to seven at the top. The local–global conjecture says the column past the horizon, the whole plane over the field, stands exactly as high as the lowest column before it. Hover anything for what it is, how solid it is, and where it is written down.

How to read this map
  1. A row is a field — the plane whose coordinates lie in it. Rows are grouped by what the primes above 2 and 3 do, then by the lowest column.
  2. A column is a place. Left: the real place (∞). Then the place above 2. Then the primes ≡ 3 (mod 4), where a field may have a finite plane. Then the primes ≡ 1 (mod 4), which are split in every field. Then the horizon — infinitely many places — and the whole plane over the field.
  3. Height is colours. A bar rises to the fewest colours that place's plane is proven to need; a dot sits where a colouring exists. Each dot is a colouring we own, and the lowest one colours the whole field.
  4. The conjecture says the last column should stand exactly at the lowest column (the small triangle). Where the bar and the dot of the last column differ, the answer is open — that is where the violet shadows live: colourings nobody owns, carved away format by format, running off past the horizon.
  5. Walls are no-go theorems. Dark and solid: standing; teal and dashed: broken, with what broke it. The dashed orange line at six crosses the window planes 23, 27, 31, 43, 47, where six is undecided.
  6. Solidity is grade: solid fill is kernel-checked, a double edge a certificate, lighter fills hand proofs, dotted outlines computed or measured, hollow a reading or open. The bars under each field's name count the theorems that mention it.
