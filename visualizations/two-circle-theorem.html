Hadwiger–Nelson · a local ceiling

Two circles, and why they can
never need a fourth colour

Pick a point. Draw the circle of radius 1 around it, and the circle of radius 1/√3. Mark as many points as you like on those two circles — a hundred, a million, all of them. Join two marks whenever they are exactly one unit apart. However you do this, the result can always be coloured with three colours so that no join has both ends the same colour.

The plane as a whole is harder: colouring every point of ℝ² so that no two points exactly 1 apart share a colour is known to need at least 5 colours and at most 7, and nobody knows the truth. This page is about a region where the answer is pinned down — and about the one loophole that pinning leaves open.

step 1 the two radii

Why these two circles and no others

The radius 1 circle is obvious: it is the set of points one unit from the centre. The second radius is the one that keeps turning up.

1/√3 ≈ 0.5774 is the circumradius of an equilateral triangle of side 1 — the distance from the triangle's centre to each of its corners. So a unit equilateral triangle inscribes exactly in that circle, and a circle of that size is the smallest one that can carry a pair of points a full unit apart at all. Any smaller and no two of its points reach that far; any point strictly between the two radii lives on neither circle and is outside this theorem.

Together they are the natural neighbourhood of a centre point p: the points at distance 1 from p, and the points that can be forced into triangles with them.

step 2 the edge census

A unit-long join can happen in only three ways

This is the whole reason the problem is tractable. Two marks a unit apart are either both outside, both inside, or one of each — and in each case the angle they subtend at the centre is forced to a single value.

outer to outer

exactly 60°

inner to inner

exactly 120°

outer to inner

exactly θ ≈ 73.221°

The first two are the equilateral triangle read twice. Two radius-1 points a unit apart form a unit equilateral triangle with the centre, so the angle at the centre is 60°. Two points on the small circle a unit apart form a unit segment whose endpoints sit on the circumcircle of a unit triangle, so they subtend 120°.

The third comes from the law of cosines and is the only number here that is not a round one. With sides 1 and 1/√3 and the opposite side equal to 1:

1 = 1 + 1/3 − 2·(1/√3)·cos θ  ⟹  cos θ = √3/6 = 0.288675…  ⟹  θ = 73.2213…°

Notice what did not happen: no fourth case. There is no unit-length join that changes the angle by anything other than ±60°, ±120°, or ±θ.

step 3 coordinates

Every mark gets a two-number address

Since a point on either circle is determined by its angle, and every join moves the angle by one of three fixed amounts, we can stop drawing circles and start counting steps.

Fix any starting mark and call its angle the origin. Every mark you can reach from it by following joins sits at an angle of the form

angle = origin + i·60° + j·θ

for some whole numbers i and j. Write the address as (i, j). Because six 60° steps come back to where they started, i only matters modulo 6 — it is a number on a six-hour clock. But j is a genuine integer that never wraps, for a reason we come to in step 6.

In this coordinate system the three kinds of join become three arithmetic rules, and the geometry is gone:

outer(i, j) — outer(i±1, j)
inner(i, j) — inner(i±2, j)
outer(i, j) — inner(i, j±1)

Each address carries two marks, one on each circle, at the same angle: the outer one and the inner one. That is why the rules mention both. The last rule is the only one that changes j, so j counts how many times you have crossed between the circles.

step 4 the shape

What those rules draw is an infinite ladder

Left: the marks where they actually live. Right: the same marks laid out by address, i down, j across. Point at either picture and the matching marks light up in both.

The ladder has six rows (the six values of i, joined top to bottom in a cycle) and runs forever left and right. Every mark has exactly four joins: two along its row-cycle and two crossing to the neighbouring columns. Nothing else is ever adjacent to anything else.

An aside worth having. The two circles hold uncountably many marks, but they do not all sit on one ladder. Starting from a different mark generally lands you in a different family that shares no joins with the first. The two circles are a heap of disjoint copies of the same ladder — so colouring one ladder correctly colours all of them.

step 5 the colouring

Two short lookup tables finish the proof

Colour a mark by looking up its i — ignoring j entirely — in one of two tables, depending on which circle it is on.

Three rules, eighteen comparisons, all of them unequal. That is the entire proof. It works for every layer at once because neither table ever consults j, so a join that crosses between columns compares outer(i) against inner(i) with the same i on both sides.

One design note that is easy to miss: the outer table uses all three colours even though the six-row cycle is even and could be alternated with two. It has to. The inner triangles need three colours among themselves, and each inner mark can only see outer marks of matching i — a two-colour outer table would starve them.

step 6 the load-bearing fact

The address has to be unique, and that needs a theorem

The tables assume each mark has one address. If one mark could be reached by two different routes with different i values, the tables would demand two colours for one point and the whole thing collapses.

That is exactly what happens if θ is a rational multiple of 60°. Here are the values of j·θ reduced modulo 60°, which is where the addresses could collide:

With the true θ the ticks never land on each other, no matter how far you go. The proof is a short piece of algebra: if some whole number of θ steps closed the loop, then 2·cos θ = √3/3 would be an algebraic integer — a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. Squaring it gives 1/3, which would then be an algebraic integer too. But a rational algebraic integer is an ordinary integer, and 1/3 is not. So no whole number of θ steps ever closes the loop, the ladder never becomes a cylinder, and j is a real integer coordinate.

This is the one place where the specific value √3/6 matters rather than just being some angle. Change the radii and the argument has to be rerun.

step 7 the loophole

Put the centre back in and three colours die

The theorem is about marks on the two circles. The centre p is not on either. Add it, and sixteen points are enough to force a fourth colour.

Nothing here contradicts step 5. The sixteen points minus p are still three-colourable — the tables still work on them. What fails is the extension: no proper three-colouring of the cover set survives adding the centre. So the honest statement of the barrier is narrower than "this region cannot force anything". It is: the two circles cannot force a fourth colour among themselves, while the centre together with them can.

Why this matters for the hunt. If you are searching for a 6-chromatic unit-distance graph by bolting gadgets onto a pivot point, this says a gadget living entirely inside a pivot's neighbourhood is wasted effort — its chromatic number is capped at 3 before you start. The leverage is in which of the three-colourings extend, and since the ladder couples only neighbouring columns, that question is a finite-state one: build the transfer matrix on column colourings and every extension question becomes reachability in a small automaton.

step 8 two systems

Bring in a second pivot, and count what the two systems share

Place a second pivot Q exactly one unit from P and give it the same two circles. Each pivot now sits on the other's outer circle, and the four circles cut each other in eight points. With the two pivots themselves, the systems share exactly ten points — no more, no fewer; the classification of all four circle-pair intersections is a kernel-checked theorem.

Now ask the ladder question. Each glue point lies in exactly one ladder of each system, so choosing one ladder per system decides which shared points you actually catch. Take the natural choice — P's ladder through Q, and Q's ladder through P — and check each glue point's angle against the ladder's angle set a·60° + b·θ:

outer∩outer  at ±60°       = a·60°         → in the ladder
seam images   at ±θ         = b·θ           → in the ladder
their mirrors at ±(180°−2θ) = 3·60° − 2θ  → in the ladder
inner∩inner   at ±30°       : 30 = a·60 + b·θ has no whole-number solution

Eight of the ten shared points come with the single ladder pair for free — the two pivots, both hexagon points, and all four seam images land in it by coset arithmetic, nothing needs to be forced. The two inner∩inner points are the exception: 30° needs b = 0 (θ is irrational) and then a = ½, which is not a whole number. They are invisible to every pivot ladder, and catching them costs a second ladder on each side — which then contains both of them at once, since they sit 60° apart.

The blind spot is an address worth knowing. That unreachable inner∩inner point (½, √3/6) is the centroid of the unit triangle on P, Q — the point one unit from neither pivot but 1/√3 from both. Add a third pivot to make the triangle and this exact point becomes the metapivot: the centre of a fourth two-circle system whose inner circle passes through all three pivots. The next stage of the construction lives precisely at the one shared point the pair's own ladders cannot see.

Now the question the picture was built to answer: restricted to one ladder per system, how strongly are the two ladders linked? Far more strongly than the shared points suggest. Because the pivots are exactly one unit apart, sliding the whole plane by that unit carries P's circles onto Q's circles — and every point is exactly one unit from its own slid copy. The slide does not rotate, so it preserves the address lattice: every mark of P's ladder has a unit-distance cross chord to the mark of Q's ladder three rows down at the same column. Switch on the cross chords and watch: this is a perfect matching — one chord per vertex, infinitely many — so for any two marks of P's ladder there is a loop out along one chord, through Q's ladder, and back along another. The colouring consequence is pointwise: a colouring of Q's ladder must differ from the slid copy of P's colouring everywhere, while agreeing with it at the six fused points. The two ladders are entangled as whole functions, not through a bottleneck.

On top of the matching there are exactly twelve sporadic chords — verified by exact field arithmetic out to column |b| ≤ 10, all twelve sitting within two seam steps of the anchor, highlighted in amber. Their chord vectors are not new directions: each one realises one of the four glue-unit vectors (the hexagon unit and the seam-image unit) anchored at a non-glue mark. These skew chords are what break the matching's parallel structure — the tight loops through them mix the two ladders' coordinates instead of running alongside them.

The probe tells the rest of the story: a point at a generic angle ψ on P's outer circle has up to four unit-distance partners on Q's circles, and exactly one of them is always its slid copy — the matching chord, staying in the corresponding ladder. The other three involve a new square root and land in other ladders of Q, different ones for every ψ. Each ladder of P touches countably many foreign ladders of Q one point at a time; that wider web is where the measured ambient colour pressure lives.
